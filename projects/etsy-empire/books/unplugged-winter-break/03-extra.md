# Chapter 19: Big Feelings When Everyone Is Home

Holidays are wonderful, and they are also intense. Routines change, sleep shifts, sugar flows freely, relatives visit, and everyone is together in the same rooms all day. It's no surprise that tempers flare, for children and adults alike. Screens are often used to calm things down. Here are other tools that work.

## Connection before correction

When a child is upset, their thinking brain is offline for a moment. Lectures don't land. What helps first is connection:

- Get down to their level.
- Name what you see: "You're really frustrated. The tower fell down again."
- Keep your voice low and slow. Your calm is borrowed by their nervous system.
- Wait. Most big feelings pass faster when they're met rather than fought.

Once they're calmer, you can talk about what happened and what to do next time.

## A calm corner

Create a cozy space where anyone, child or adult, can go to settle: a beanbag or cushions, a soft blanket, a few books, a squishy toy, headphones with calm music, a picture card with breathing ideas. It's not a punishment spot. It's a place to come back to yourself.

## Breathing games for kids

Children rarely respond to "calm down and breathe." They do respond to play:

- **Birthday candles:** hold up five fingers and blow out each "candle" slowly.
- **Hot cocoa breath:** smell the cocoa (breathe in through the nose), cool it down (breathe out through the mouth).
- **Balloon belly:** put a hand on the tummy and fill it like a balloon, then let the air out slowly.
- **Snake breath:** breathe in, then breathe out with a long "sssss."

Practice these when everyone is calm, so they're familiar when they're needed.

## Movement resets

Sometimes a body needs to move to feel better. Try a dance break, ten jumping jacks, a run to the end of the garden and back, or a "shake it out" song. Many meltdowns in the late afternoon are simply bodies that haven't moved enough.

## When you lose your temper

You will, at some point. Every parent does. What matters most is repair:

> "I'm sorry I shouted. I was frustrated, but shouting wasn't okay. Can we try again?"

Repair teaches children that relationships can survive hard moments, and that grown-ups take responsibility too.

# Chapter 20: Siblings, Squabbles and Sharing Space

More time together often means more arguments. That doesn't mean the break is going badly. Siblings are learning to negotiate, share and solve problems, and that learning is noisy.

## Prevent the predictable fights

- **Give each child a "mine" space** for special toys that don't have to be shared.
- **Use a timer for turns** with popular toys or games.
- **Plan one-on-one time** with each child, even 15 minutes a day. Many sibling fights are really a bid for a parent's attention.
- **Separate before it escalates:** "I can see this is getting hard. Let's take a break in different rooms for ten minutes."

## Coach, don't judge

When you weren't there to see who started it, avoid playing referee. Instead, describe the problem and hand it back:

> "I see two kids and one scooter. That's a tricky problem. What ideas do you have?"

Children often come up with surprisingly fair solutions when they're trusted to try.

## Team challenges

Activities that require cooperation turn rivals into teammates: building one big fort together, a joint treasure hunt, a two-person cooking project, or a "beat the timer" tidy-up race against the clock rather than each other.

# Chapter 21: Rainy Weeks and Sick Days

Some breaks don't go to plan. A stomach bug, a week of rain, a parent with the flu. On these days, it's absolutely fine for screens to play a bigger role. Rest matters more than rules.

## When a child is sick

- Let them rest. Sleep and quiet are the priority.
- Gentle activities help when they're bored but not well: audiobooks, sticker books, simple puzzles, coloring, listening to music, being read to.
- Screens can be a comfort. Choose calm, slower shows and keep the volume low.
- Return to the usual rhythm gradually once they're feeling better.

## When you're sick

Lower every standard. Order food if you can. Ask for help. Put on a film, set out snacks and a busy box, and rest on the sofa nearby. Your children will remember being cared for, not how many hours of screen time they had.

## A rainy-week emergency kit

Keep a box ready for days when you're stuck inside: a new craft kit, a pack of play dough, a new deck of cards, a puzzle, painter's tape for floor games, a few new library books, and a list of indoor games in the 100-activity list.

# Chapter 22: Back to School Without the Battle

The last few days of the break can be bumpy, especially if sleep and routines have drifted. A gentle transition makes the first school morning much easier.

## Three days before school

- **Shift bedtime back** by 15–20 minutes each evening.
- **Bring back the school-day rhythm:** breakfast at the usual time, getting dressed early.
- **Talk about what's coming:** friends they'll see, things they're looking forward to, anything they're worried about.

## The night before

- Pack bags and lay out clothes together.
- Keep the evening calm and screen-free.
- Read the best-of-break memory book you made together.

## Keep one thing from the break

Ask the family: "What's one thing from this break we want to keep?" Maybe it's Friday fort night, a weekly game evening, or phone-free dinners. Write it down and put it on the fridge.

# Chapter 23: Screen-Free Evenings and Calmer Bedtimes

Evenings are often when screens creep back in. Everyone is tired, dinner needs making, and a show seems like the easiest way through. But screens in the last hour before bed tend to make settling harder: the content is exciting, the light is bright, and stopping often triggers protest just when everyone needs calm.

## A wind-down rhythm

Try a simple, predictable sequence that children can learn by heart:

1. **Tidy-up song** (five minutes, everyone helps)
2. **Bath or wash**
3. **Pajamas and teeth**
4. **A warm drink or small snack**
5. **Two stories** (or one chapter for older children)
6. **One question to talk about:** "What was the best part of your day?" "What made you laugh?"
7. **Lights low, a soft song or audiobook**

The order matters less than the repetition. When the steps are always the same, children know what's coming and resist less.

## Evening activities that calm instead of excite

- Drawing or coloring at the kitchen table
- Puzzles
- Reading together on the sofa
- Gentle stretching or "animal yoga"
- Listening to an audiobook in the dark
- Looking at family photo albums
- Writing in the best-of-break memory book

## When older children want their devices at night

For tweens, agree that phones and tablets "sleep" outside the bedroom, charging in the kitchen. Present it as a family rule that adults follow too, rather than a punishment. Offer an alarm clock and a book light so the phone isn't needed.

# Chapter 24: New Family Traditions Without Screens

The best holiday memories are rarely of what was on a screen. They're of the smell of cookies, the cold air on a night walk, a silly game that made everyone cry with laughter. Traditions give children something to look forward to every year, and they're easy to start.

## Ideas to try

- **The first-snow cocoa:** whenever the first snow falls, everyone drops what they're doing for hot chocolate.
- **Christmas Eve box:** pajamas, a book and a treat to open on Christmas Eve.
- **A family lights walk:** a yearly walk or drive to see the best-decorated houses.
- **Kindness advent:** one small kind act each day in December.
- **Cookie decorating night** with music and too many sprinkles.
- **The family "year in review":** on New Year's Eve, each person shares their favorite memory of the year.
- **Gratitude chain:** a paper chain where each link holds something you're thankful for.
- **New Year's Day walk:** start the year outdoors, together.

## Making a tradition stick

Choose just one or two. Do them every year, at the same time, in the same way. Give them a name. Take a photo each year in the same spot. Children love seeing how much they've grown.

# Chapter 25: Questions Parents Often Ask

**"My child says everyone else gets unlimited screen time. What do I say?"**
Stay calm and kind: "Every family makes its own rules. In our family, we have screen time after lunch, and lots of other fun things too." You don't need to defend your choice or criticize other families. Consistency matters more than winning the argument.

**"What if my child just sits and does nothing when the screen goes off?"**
That's okay. Doing nothing is often the step before doing something. Resist filling the gap immediately. Leave inviting materials nearby and give it ten to fifteen minutes. If they're still stuck, offer a choice of two activities rather than an open question.

**"Isn't some screen time educational?"**
Some is, especially when it's chosen with intention and talked about afterwards. The problem is rarely one good documentary; it's the endless stream that follows it. Choose, watch, switch off, then do something with what you learned.

**"My teenager needs their phone to stay in touch with friends. Is that okay?"**
Yes. Friendships are important, and for many teens messaging is how they connect. Agree on times and phone-free zones together rather than banning the phone. Invite friends over for real-world plans, too.

**"I work from home. Is this realistic for me?"**
Yes, with a different plan. Read the working parent's chapter: planned screen time during your most important hours, independent play stations, short connection breaks and shared childcare. Aim for a better balance, not perfection.

**"What if grandparents give the kids tablets as Christmas presents?"**
Thank them warmly. Then decide where the tablet lives and when it's used, just like any other device in your home. A new device doesn't need to change your family's rhythm.

**"We tried, and the first day was a disaster. Should we give up?"**
The first days are almost always the hardest. Keep the rhythm, lower your expectations, and look for small wins. Most families notice a real shift after about three to four days.

**"How do I handle my own phone habits?"**
Gently, without guilt. Pick one or two changes, like phone-free meals or a phone parking spot, and tell your children what you're doing. They'll notice, and it makes your rules feel fair.

**"Is it bad to use a screen when I'm exhausted?"**
No. A show that gives you a quiet half hour is a reasonable tool, especially on hard days. The goal is a break that feels balanced overall, not a perfect score every day.

**"When should I worry?"**
If screen use comes with extreme distress, withdrawal from friends and activities, serious sleep problems, or signs that something upsetting happened online, talk to your child's doctor or a family professional. Reaching out early is always okay.

# A Final Word

You picked up this book because you wanted something better for your children's time off: more laughter, more play, more of the kind of memories that stay. That wish is already the most important part.

Some days of the break will be magical. Some will be loud, messy and full of complaints. Both are part of a real family holiday. What your children will remember is not a perfect plan, but the feeling of a parent who chose to be there with them, building the fort, losing at cards, sweeping up the flour.

Thank you for giving them that. Their wonder was never gone. It just needed a little space.

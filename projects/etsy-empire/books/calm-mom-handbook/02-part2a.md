# Part Two: Calmer Days

The tools in Part One help you in the moment. Part Two is about making those moments less frequent. Most yelling happens in the same handful of situations, day after day. When those situations get a plan, the whole household gets calmer, and you get your patience back for the moments that truly need it.

Each chapter in this part follows the same structure: why the moment is so hard, a step-by-step plan, word-for-word scripts by age and a troubleshooting section for the days when the plan falls apart.

# Chapter 5: Connection Before Correction

Here is a pattern almost every parent recognizes. You ask your child to do something. They ignore you. You ask again, a little louder. They argue. You warn. They whine. Within a few minutes, a simple request has turned into a power struggle, and you're yelling.

One of the most reliable ways to break this pattern sounds almost too simple: **connect first, then correct.** Before you ask a child to change what they're doing, take five to ten seconds to connect with them. It feels slower. It's actually much faster.

## Why connection works

Children cooperate with people they feel connected to. This isn't a parenting trick; it's how all humans work. Think about how differently you respond to a request from a friend who greets you warmly versus a stranger who barks at you from across a room.

When a child is deep in play, their attention is fully absorbed. A request shouted from the kitchen often doesn't even register as being about them. When you walk over, get down to their level, touch their shoulder and show interest in what they're doing, you shift their attention to you. Their brain has a moment to switch tracks. Only then does your request have a real chance.

Connection also lowers the "threat" level for both of you. A calm, close voice tells your child's nervous system that they are safe, which makes them far more able to listen, and tells your own body that this is a manageable moment, not an emergency.

## The connection sequence (10 seconds)

1. **Go to them.** Stop calling from another room. Walk over. Yes, every time, at least for the first few weeks.
2. **Get to their level.** Crouch, kneel or sit next to them.
3. **Make gentle contact.** A hand on the shoulder or back, if your child likes touch. Eye contact if they're comfortable with it.
4. **Join their world for a moment.** Comment on what they're doing: *"Wow, that tower is huge."* *"Is that the level with the dragon?"*
5. **Then make the request.** Short and clear: *"In two minutes, it's time for bath. Do you want to finish the tower or knock it down?"*

That's it. It takes about ten seconds and can prevent a ten-minute battle.

## What to say: connection openers

You don't need to be clever. Interest and warmth are enough.

> "Tell me about what you're building."

> "I love how focused you are."

> "Which character is that?"

> "Show me the best part."

> "You two look like you're having fun."

For older children, keep it low-key. Big enthusiasm can feel fake to a ten-year-old. A simple *"What are you watching?"* or *"Did you beat that level?"* is often enough.

## Give warnings and choices

Two simple additions make the connection sequence even stronger.

**Warnings.** Sudden stops feel unfair to children (and to adults). A warning gives the brain time to prepare. For younger children, time is abstract, so tie the warning to something concrete:

> "Two more slides, then we go home."

> "When this song ends, it's teeth time."

> "Finish this page, then dinner."

**Choices.** Children feel more cooperative when they have some control. Give two choices, both of which are fine with you:

> "Do you want to walk to the bath or hop like a bunny?"

> "Shoes first or jacket first?"

> "Do you want to set the forks or the cups?"

Choices are not bribes or negotiations. You've already decided *what* will happen; the child gets to choose a small part of *how*. This tiny bit of control often melts resistance before it starts.

## Ten minutes a day that change everything

Connection isn't only something you use right before a request. It's also something you build up over the day, like a bank account. When the account is full, children are more patient with your requests. When it's empty, they push and test far more.

One of the simplest ways to fill the account is **special time**: ten minutes a day of one-on-one play, where your child leads and you follow. No phones, no teaching, no correcting. Just you, fully present, doing what they choose.

How to do special time:

- **Name it.** *"It's our special time! You choose what we play."*
- **Set a timer** for 10 minutes so you can give it your full attention without worrying about the rest of your day.
- **Follow their lead.** If they want to play dragons, you're a dragon. If they want to build, you build what they ask.
- **Describe and enjoy.** *"You're putting the red block on top. Ooh, now it's wobbling!"*
- **Avoid questions that test or teach.** This isn't the time for *"What color is that?"* or *"Count the blocks."*
- **End warmly.** *"I loved our special time. Let's do it again tomorrow."*

Many parents are amazed by how much this changes behavior across the whole day. Children who feel deeply seen for ten minutes often have less need to demand attention through whining, clinging or acting out. If you have several children, try to give each one their own special time, even if it's just five minutes, a few times a week.

## Composite example: Sam's "I'm not listening" son

Sam's five-year-old, Leo, seemed to have selective hearing. Sam would call "Leo, dinner!" from the kitchen five times before finally yelling it, and Leo would come, upset and crying, accusing his mother of being mean.

Sam tried the connection sequence for one week. Each time, she walked to the living room, knelt beside Leo, put a hand on his back and said something like *"Is that the fire truck? Is it going to the fire?"* Then: *"The fire truck can park here. It's dinner time. Do you want to drive it to the table or leave it in the garage?"*

On the first day, it felt painfully slow. By the end of the week, Sam noticed that Leo came to dinner on the first request most of the time, and evenings were far calmer. She was spending perhaps an extra minute walking to him each time, and saving fifteen minutes of shouting and tears.

## When connection isn't enough

Connection makes cooperation much more likely, but it doesn't guarantee it. Some days your child will still say no, ignore you or melt down. That's normal. When connection doesn't work, the next step is a **calm, firm limit**, which is exactly what the next chapter is about.

The important shift is this: you are no longer jumping straight from a request to yelling. You now have a sequence:

1. Connect.
2. Warn and offer a choice.
3. If needed, set a calm, firm limit and follow through.

There's no "yell" step. The yell has been replaced by action.

## If it doesn't work

- **"I don't have time to walk over every time."** In the beginning, it really does take a bit more time. Think of it as an investment. Once cooperation improves, you'll find you need to ask far less often, and you'll get that time back many times over.
- **"My child still ignores me after I connect."** Make sure your request is short and specific (*"Shoes on now"* rather than *"We need to get ready, okay?"*). Then move to the calm-limit steps in Chapter 6.
- **"Special time feels impossible with three kids."** Shorter, rotating special time works too: five minutes each, or one child per day on a rotating schedule. Even a few minutes of full attention a few times a week makes a difference.
- **"My child uses special time to demand more and more."** End warmly but firmly: *"Special time is over for today. I loved it. We'll have it again tomorrow."* Consistency helps children trust that it will come back, which reduces the clinging over time.
- **"I'm too drained for play."** That's a real and common feeling. On very low-capacity days, choose the calmest possible special time: reading together on the couch, coloring side by side or lying on the floor while your child shows you their toys. Presence counts more than energy.

# Chapter 16: Refilling the Tank

Remember the window of tolerance from Chapter 2? Everything that drains you narrows it: short sleep, skipped meals, noise, worry, and the endless list of things only you seem to remember. Everything that refills you widens it again. This chapter is about the refilling part, in the small, realistic doses that fit into a real family day.

## Why "self-care" often fails

Many mothers roll their eyes at the word self-care. It conjures up spa days and long baths, which can feel impossible with small children and a full calendar. When self-care sounds like one more task on the list, it rarely happens.

A more useful way to think about it is maintenance. You don't wait for your car to break down before you put fuel in it. Your nervous system works the same way. Small, regular refills keep it running; skipping them until the tank is empty almost guarantees a breakdown, and for many parents a breakdown looks like yelling.

## The four basics

Before anything else, check the basics. They are boring and they matter more than any technique in this book.

**Sleep.** Short sleep makes almost everyone more irritable and less patient. You may not be able to get eight hours with a baby or a toddler who wakes at night, but you can protect what you have. Go to bed when the children do, two nights a week. Trade nights with a partner. Leave the dishes.

**Food.** Low blood sugar shortens tempers fast. Many mothers feed everyone else and skip their own meals. Keep a snack in your bag, your car and the kitchen drawer, and eat before the hardest part of the day, not after it.

**Movement and air.** A ten-minute walk outside with the stroller or the dog changes the body's stress level more than ten minutes of scrolling. It counts even if the kids come along.

**Connection.** Loneliness is one of the most underrated drains on patience. One honest conversation with a friend, a text to someone who gets it or a coffee with another parent can refill more than you'd expect.

## Micro-refills: five minutes or less

You won't always get an hour. You can almost always get a few minutes.

- Drink a full glass of water and look out of the window.
- Step outside and take five slow breaths.
- Put on one song you love and let yourself sing.
- Sit in the car for three minutes before going into the house.
- Lie on the floor while the kids play around you.
- Text one friend: "Long day. How's yours?"
- Close the bathroom door and do the Two-Breath Pause three times.

Pick two that suit you and attach them to moments that already happen every day: after school pick-up, before you start dinner, after the kids are in bed.

## Share the load

A lot of what drains you is work nobody sees: the planning, remembering and worrying about whose shoes are too small, when the vaccinations are due, what to bring to the class party. When one person carries all of that, they carry it every hour of the day, even while resting.

You can't make the invisible load disappear, but you can make it visible and share some of it. Write down everything you keep track of for one week. Then pick one whole area (school forms, birthday presents, the pediatrician) and hand it over completely, including the remembering. Chapter 15 has words for that conversation.

## What to say when you need a break

To your partner:

> "I'm at my limit. I need twenty minutes alone. Can you take over until then?"

To your children (yes, really):

> "I'm getting grumpy because I'm tired. I'm going to sit down with my tea for five minutes. Then I'll be a nicer mom."

Children learn a lot from seeing a parent take care of herself before she snaps. You are showing them what to do with their own big feelings later.

## Composite example: Maya's four-thirty snack

Maya, a mother of three, noticed from her Trigger Map that almost all of her yelling happened between 4:30 and 6 p.m. When she looked closer, she realized that she usually ate lunch at noon and nothing else until the children were in bed. She started eating a proper snack at 4:15 and taking five minutes outside after the school run. Nothing else changed. Within two weeks, her husband asked what was different. "I'm not starving anymore," she said, half laughing. It wasn't the whole answer, but it removed a surprisingly large share of her hardest afternoons.

## If it doesn't work

- **"I don't have five minutes."** Start with one minute. A glass of water and two slow breaths still count. Then look honestly at the load: if there is truly no gap anywhere in your day, something needs to change, and that is a conversation to have with your partner, your family or a professional.
- **"I feel guilty taking time for myself."** Your children benefit directly when you are less depleted. Rest is part of parenting, not a break from it.
- **"Nothing refills me anymore."** If nothing feels enjoyable, or you feel flat, numb or hopeless for more than two weeks, it may be depression or burnout. Please read Chapter 17.

# Chapter 17: When It's More Than Yelling

Most of this book is for the common situation: a loving parent who loses her temper more than she wants to. This chapter is for the moments when something more might be going on, either with you or with your child. Reaching out for help in these moments is responsible and brave. It is one of the most loving things you can do for your family.

## Signs it's time to talk to a professional about you

Consider talking to your doctor, midwife or a mental health professional if you notice any of these, especially if they last longer than two weeks:

- You feel sad, empty, hopeless or numb most of the day.
- You feel anxious, on edge or panicky much of the time.
- Your anger feels out of control, frightening or explosive, even after trying the tools in this book.
- You have scary, intrusive thoughts, including thoughts of harming yourself or your child.
- You can't sleep even when you have the chance, or you sleep far more than usual.
- You've lost interest in things you used to enjoy.
- You feel disconnected from your baby or your children.
- You're using alcohol or other substances to cope.

After pregnancy and birth, many parents experience depression or anxiety, and it can appear months later, not only in the first weeks. It's common, it's treatable, and it's not your fault.

## If you are in crisis right now

If you are thinking about harming yourself or someone else, or you feel unsafe, please get help now.

- **US:** Call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or call **911** in an emergency.
- **National Maternal Mental Health Hotline (US):** call or text **1-833-852-6262** (1-833-TLC-MAMA), free and confidential, for pregnant and new parents.
- **Postpartum Support International HelpLine (US):** **1-800-944-4773**.
- **If you are afraid you might hurt your child:** the **Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline (US), 1-800-422-4453**, offers confidential support to parents too.
- **UK:** Samaritans, **116 123**, free, any time.
- **Elsewhere:** contact your local emergency number or your doctor.

You don't have to be at a breaking point to call. Many of these services exist for exactly the moment when you feel you're barely holding on.

## Signs it's time to talk to someone about your child

Every child has hard days, tantrums and phases. Talk to your pediatrician or family doctor if:

- tantrums are very frequent, very long or getting more intense as your child gets older,
- your child often hurts themselves, other people or animals,
- worries or fears stop your child from going to school, sleeping or playing,
- your child seems withdrawn, sad or not themselves for weeks,
- a teacher or caregiver raises concerns you also recognize,
- your gut tells you something isn't right.

Some children have needs that make everyday life harder for everyone, such as anxiety, ADHD, sensory differences or developmental delays. An assessment helps you find out what your child needs, and it often brings huge relief to the whole family.

## What kind of help is there?

- **Your family doctor or pediatrician:** a good first stop for both your health and your child's.
- **Therapists and counselors:** individual therapy for you, family therapy for the household, or parent coaching focused on behavior.
- **Parenting programs:** structured, evidence-based programs are available in many areas, often through health services or schools.
- **Support groups:** local or online groups for parents, including groups for postpartum depression and anxiety.

## What to say when you reach out

It can be hard to find words. You can use these:

> "I've been feeling really overwhelmed, and I'm losing my temper with my kids more than I want to. I'd like some help."

> "Since my baby was born, I haven't felt like myself. I'm worried it might be postpartum depression."

> "I'm worried about my child's behavior, and I don't know if it's normal. Can we talk about it?"

## A final word on this chapter

If you read this chapter and recognized yourself, I hope you'll take one step today: one phone call, one appointment, one honest message to someone you trust. You deserve support. Your children deserve a mom who gets it. And none of this means you have failed. It means you are taking your family's wellbeing seriously.

## If it doesn't work

- **"I tried to get help and it wasn't helpful."** Please try again. Not every professional is the right fit. It's fine to ask for a different person or a second opinion.
- **"There's a long waiting list."** Ask to be put on it anyway, and in the meantime use helplines, support groups and your doctor.
- **"I'm afraid they'll judge me or take my children."** Professionals who work with parents see struggling, loving parents every day. Asking for help is a sign of a parent who cares.

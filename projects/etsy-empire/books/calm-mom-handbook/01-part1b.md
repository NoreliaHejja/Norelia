# Chapter 4: Repair – What to Do After You Yell

![](ch4.jpg)

You will yell again. Probably this week. Maybe today.

That is not pessimism; it's honesty. Even with every tool in this book, you are a human being raising other human beings in a loud, demanding world. The goal was never zero yelling. The goal is that yelling becomes rarer, shorter and, most importantly, followed by **repair**.

If you take only one chapter from this book seriously, make it this one. Repair is the single most powerful thing you can do after a hard moment, and almost nobody teaches it.

## Why repair matters so much

Researchers who study relationships between babies, children and their caregivers have long noticed something comforting: no relationship is perfectly in tune all the time. Even very loving parents miss signals, get frustrated and disconnect many times a day. What seems to matter most is not avoiding every break in connection, but what happens *after* the break. When a parent comes back, reconnects and makes things right, the child learns something enormously valuable:

- Big feelings can be survived.
- People who love each other can hurt each other and still be okay.
- Mistakes can be owned and fixed.
- I am safe, even when things go wrong.

This idea is often called **"rupture and repair."** The rupture is the yell, the slammed door, the harsh word. The repair is the return. A relationship with many ruptures and no repairs feels unsafe. A relationship with ruptures that are reliably repaired can actually become *stronger*, because the child learns that the connection is sturdy enough to survive conflict.

One important warning: repair is not a way to erase anything. Repair is not "I can yell as much as I want as long as I say sorry." Children notice when apologies are followed by the same thing again and again, and the apology loses its meaning. Real repair includes a genuine effort to do better. That's what the rest of this book is for.

## What repair is not

Before we look at what to do, let's clear away a few things that look like repair but aren't:

- **The "sorry, but" apology.** *"I'm sorry I yelled, but you were being impossible."* The "but" erases the sorry. Your child hears: "It was your fault."
- **The guilt flood.** *"I'm the worst mom ever, I'm so sorry, I'm terrible, I don't deserve you…"* This puts your child in the position of having to comfort *you*. It's too heavy for them.
- **Buying forgiveness.** Ice cream, a new toy or an extra hour of screens to make up for a yell teaches children that big feelings are followed by treats, and it skips the real conversation.
- **Pretending it didn't happen.** Moving on as if nothing happened leaves children alone with their confusion. Many children then fill the silence with the worst explanation: *"It was because I'm bad."*

## The four steps of repair

Good repair is simple. It has four parts, and it can take less than a minute.

**1. Calm yourself first.** You cannot repair while you're still boiling. Use the Two-Breath Pause, step away, drink some water. If you need ten minutes, take ten minutes. You can say: *"I need a few minutes to calm my body. Then I want to talk to you."* Then come back. Coming back is the most important part.

**2. Name what you did, without excuses.** Be specific and short. *"I yelled at you when you spilled your juice."* Not *"Mommy got a little upset,"* which is vague, and not *"You made me so angry,"* which blames the child.

**3. Take responsibility and reassure.** *"That wasn't okay. It's my job to stay calm, even when I'm frustrated. You didn't deserve to be yelled at. I love you, always, even when I'm angry."*

**4. Look forward together.** *"Next time I feel that angry, I'm going to take my breaths. And next time the juice spills, we can grab a towel together."* If there's still a behavior issue to address (and there often is), address it now, calmly, as a separate step.

Notice what you are *not* doing: you're not saying the child's behavior was fine. If your child hit their sister and then you yelled, both things can be true: *"I'm sorry I yelled. Yelling wasn't okay. And hitting isn't okay either. Let's talk about what you can do next time you're that angry."* You are owning your part and still holding the limit.

## Repair scripts by age

Children understand repair differently depending on their age. Here are ready-to-borrow words. Change them so they sound like you.

### Toddlers and preschoolers (2–4)

Keep it short, warm and physical. Little ones understand tone and touch more than words.

> "I used a big, loud voice. That was scary. I'm sorry. I'm calm now. Hug?"

> "Mommy yelled. Mommy's sorry. You're safe. I love you."

You can also repair through play: *"Let's be two little bears who had a grumpy moment. Grrr! Now we're friendly bears again."* Pretend play is one of the most natural ways small children process feelings.

### Young children (5–7)

Name the feeling, own the action and include a simple plan.

> "When you didn't come for dinner, I got really frustrated and I yelled. That wasn't fair to you. It's my job to handle my angry feelings. I'm sorry. Next time I'm going to come and touch your shoulder instead of yelling across the house."

Children this age often like to be part of the solution:

> "What could help us next time? Should we have a special signal for 'dinner is ready'?"

### Older children (8–12)

Older children appreciate honesty and being treated as capable. They also notice when you're being sincere, so keep it real.

> "I want to talk about this morning. I lost my temper and yelled at you about the homework. I was stressed about being late for work, and I took it out on you. That's not okay, and I'm sorry. I still want the homework done on time, but I want to figure that out with you without shouting. Can we come up with a plan together?"

Be prepared for an eye-roll or a "Whatever." That's fine. Older children often act unimpressed while quietly taking in every word. Don't push for a big emotional response. The repair still landed.

### Teens (a quick note)

If you also have a teenager, the same steps apply, but keep it brief and private. Teens can be very sensitive to embarrassment. A short, sincere *"Hey, I was out of line earlier. I'm sorry. I'm working on it,"* said in the car or by text, can mean a great deal.

## Repairing with your partner or co-parent watching

Sometimes the hardest part of repair is doing it in front of another adult, especially a partner or grandparent who may already be critical. You might feel defensive, or you might feel like apologizing to your child makes you look weak.

It doesn't. Repairing in front of others is a powerful model of maturity. You're showing everyone in the room that strong people own their mistakes. If an adult comments negatively, you can say calmly: *"I'm teaching the kids that we fix things when we mess up."* Then let it go.

## When your child won't accept the repair

Sometimes your child turns away, says "Go away!" or stays angry. This is hard, especially when you've worked up the courage to apologize. But it's normal and healthy. Your child is allowed to have their feelings about what happened.

Try:

> "That's okay. You're allowed to still be upset. I'll be right here when you're ready."

Then really be there. Stay nearby, do something calm and let them come to you. Many children circle back within minutes, or at bedtime, when they feel safe enough to reconnect. Repair isn't a transaction where you say the words and get forgiveness. It's an open door you leave open.

## When the same thing keeps happening

If you find yourself repairing the same moment again and again (every morning, every bedtime), that's not a sign that repair isn't working. It's a sign that the trigger needs a plan, not just an apology. Go back to your Trigger Map (Chapter 3), choose that situation and work through the four planning questions. Part Two of this book has detailed plans for the most common ones: mornings, bedtime, siblings, transitions and big feelings.

You might even involve your child in the plan:

> "I've noticed I've been yelling a lot in the mornings, and I don't like it. I bet you don't either. What do you think makes mornings so hard? What could help?"

You may be surprised by their ideas. Children often know exactly what's going wrong; they're just rarely asked.

## Repairing with yourself

There is one more repair that matters, and it's the one most mothers skip: the repair with yourself.

After a yell, the inner voice can be brutal. *"You're just like your mother. You're ruining them. Other moms don't do this."* That voice feels like it's motivating you to do better, but in reality it usually does the opposite. Shame drains energy, narrows your window even further and makes the next yell more likely.

Self-compassion is not letting yourself off the hook. It's treating yourself the way you would treat a friend who made the same mistake: honestly, but kindly. A simple self-compassion practice has three parts:

1. **Notice:** *"That was a really hard moment. I yelled and I feel awful."*
2. **Remember you're not alone:** *"Many, many parents struggle with this. I'm not the only one."*
3. **Offer kindness:** *"I'm learning. I can repair. I can try again tomorrow."*

You might put a hand on your heart while you say this. It sounds simple, and it is. It's also one of the most effective ways to stop the shame spiral that keeps so many mothers stuck. We'll go deeper into self-compassion in Chapter 13.

## Composite example: Priya's bedtime repair

Priya yelled at her seven-year-old, Arjun, after the fourth trip out of bed: *"I SAID GO TO SLEEP!"* He cried himself to sleep. Priya sat on the stairs feeling sick.

In the past, she would have said nothing the next morning, hoping he'd forgotten. This time, she sat on his bed at breakfast and said:

> "I want to say sorry about last night. I yelled really loudly when you came out of your room. I was very tired, but that's not your fault, and you didn't deserve to be yelled at. I love you so much. Tonight, let's make a plan so bedtime is easier for both of us."

Arjun looked at her and said, "I was scared you were still mad." Priya hugged him and said, "I'm not mad. I was frustrated, and now I'm not." That night they made a "bedtime ticket" plan (you'll find it in Chapter 8). Arjun came out once instead of four times. Priya didn't yell.

The repair didn't just fix the night before. It opened the door to solving the problem.

## The repair checklist

Use this quick list on hard days. You don't have to do it perfectly. Even a short, imperfect repair is much better than none.

- I calmed my body first.
- I came back to my child.
- I named what I did without blaming them.
- I took responsibility and reassured them.
- I made a simple plan for next time.
- I addressed any behavior separately and calmly.
- I was kind to myself afterwards.

## If it doesn't work

- **"I'm too ashamed to apologize."** Start with the smallest version: *"I'm sorry I yelled. I love you."* You can say more later. Shame shrinks when we act against it.
- **"My child uses the apology against me: 'You yelled, so I don't have to listen!'"** Calmly separate the two: *"You're right, I yelled, and I apologized. And you still need to brush your teeth. Both things are true."*
- **"I keep apologizing and nothing changes."** That's a sign to focus on prevention. Pick the trigger that causes the most repairs and use the plans in Part Two.
- **"My parents never apologized to me, and it feels strange."** It will feel strange, maybe even uncomfortable. You're doing something new, and new things often feel awkward before they feel natural. Many mothers find that apologizing to their own children slowly heals something in themselves too.

## A short summary of Part One

Before we move on to calmer daily routines, here is everything from Part One on one page:

1. **Pause.** Two slow breaths, mouth closed, long breath out. Then speak low and slow.
2. **Understand your window.** Yelling happens when your capacity is low. Protect it with food, rest, breaks and support.
3. **Map your triggers.** Find your top situation and your top inner sentence, then plan for them.
4. **Repair, every time.** Calm down, name it, own it, reassure, plan. Then be kind to yourself.

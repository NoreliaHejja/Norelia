# Chapter 9: When Siblings Fight

Few things push a parent over the edge faster than the sound of siblings fighting: the screaming, the "She started it!", the crash from the other room. Sibling conflict is one of the most common triggers for yelling, partly because it's loud, partly because it's constant and partly because it often feels like you're refereeing a match that never ends.

Here's a reframe that helps many parents: **sibling conflict is normal, and it's also practice.** Siblings are often the first people children learn to share with, negotiate with, compete with and make up with. You can't prevent every argument, and you don't need to. Your job is to keep everyone safe and slowly teach them how to solve problems without you.

## Why siblings fight

- **Competition for attention.** Children often fight more when they feel they're getting less of you.
- **Boredom and tiredness.** Late afternoons and long car rides are classic fight zones.
- **Different ages and needs.** A four-year-old who wants to play and a nine-year-old who wants to be left alone will clash.
- **Learning limits.** Children test what's allowed with each other, just as they do with you.
- **Copying.** Children often handle conflict the way they see adults handle it, including yelling.

That last point is uncomfortable but important. When we yell at children to stop yelling at each other, we're modeling exactly what we want them to stop.

## Three levels of sibling conflict

Not every argument needs the same response. Think of three levels:

**Level 1: Bickering.** Annoying but safe: "Stop copying me!", "That's mine!" **Response: stay out of it,** or offer a light comment: *"I trust you two to work it out."* Often, an audience makes it worse.

**Level 2: Heating up.** Voices rising, someone close to tears, but no one is hurting anyone. **Response: coach.** Go in calmly, describe what you see and help them find a solution.

**Level 3: Unsafe.** Hitting, kicking, throwing, real cruelty. **Response: step in immediately and separate,** calmly and firmly. Problem-solving comes later, when everyone is calm.

Knowing the level helps you avoid jumping straight to a yell for what is really just Level 1 noise.

## Coaching a Level 2 conflict

When things are heating up, try this simple sequence:

1. **Describe what you see, without blame.** *"I see two kids who both want the blue car."*
2. **Reflect each child's feelings.** *"Sam, you were playing with it first. Lily, you really want a turn."*
3. **State the problem.** *"Two kids and one blue car. That's a tough problem."*
4. **Ask for solutions.** *"What could you do?"*
5. **Step back.** Let them try. Offer an idea only if they're stuck: *"Some kids use a timer for turns."*

You're not deciding who's right. You're handing the problem back to them, with a little support. Children who are helped to solve problems again and again slowly need you less.

## Handling a Level 3 conflict calmly

When someone is getting hurt, act quickly but quietly:

> "Stop. I won't let you hurt each other. You go to the couch, you go to the kitchen table."

Then help the hurt child first:

> "Are you okay? That must have hurt."

Once everyone is calm, which may take a few minutes, come back to it:

> "Hitting is never okay. What were you trying to tell your brother? What could you do next time instead?"

Notice: no yelling, no long lecture, no forced instant apology. Children who are pushed to say sorry before they've calmed down often say it without meaning it. A real repair later is worth much more.

## Reducing fights before they start

- **One-on-one time.** Even ten minutes of special time with each child (Chapter 5) reduces the need to compete for your attention.
- **Separate spaces.** Give each child a place where their things are safe from siblings.
- **Avoid comparisons.** *"Why can't you be more like your sister?"* fuels rivalry for years.
- **Plan for fight zones.** If the car is a battlefield, plan audiobooks, seat rotations or snacks in advance.
- **Notice the good moments.** *"I noticed you shared your crayons with your brother. That was kind."* Children tend to repeat what gets noticed.

## What to say

> "I see two upset kids. Let's take a breath, then figure this out."

> "I'm not going to decide who started it. I'm going to help you solve it."

> "You both want a turn. What's a fair way to share?"

> "I won't let you hurt each other. Let's take a break apart."

## If it doesn't work

- **"They fight all day long."** Check the basics: tiredness, hunger, boredom and one-on-one time. Constant fighting is often a sign that children are running on empty, just like parents.
- **"One child is always the aggressor."** Look for patterns: when, where and what happens just before. Spend extra one-on-one time with that child. If aggression is frequent or intense, talk to your pediatrician.
- **"They won't solve problems without me."** It takes practice. Keep coaching. The goal is progress, not independence by next week.
- **"I can't stay calm when they fight."** That's completely understandable. Use the Two-Breath Pause before you walk into the room. You're allowed to take ten seconds before stepping in, unless someone is getting hurt.

# Chapter 10: Transitions and Screens

"Five more minutes!" "Just let me finish this level!" "But I'm not done!"

Transitions, moving from one activity to another, are hard for children. And the hardest transition of all, for many families, is turning off a screen. Screens are designed to be engaging, and stopping them can trigger some of the biggest meltdowns of the day. That makes screen time a prime moment for yelling.

## Why transitions are so hard

Children live very much in the present. When they're absorbed in play or a show, stopping feels like losing something. Their brains are still learning how to shift attention, handle disappointment and plan ahead. Add tiredness or hunger, and even a small transition can become a battle.

Screens add another layer. Many games and apps are built to keep people engaged: autoplay, levels, rewards. Stopping them is genuinely hard, even for adults. When your child melts down after the tablet goes off, they're struggling with something that was designed to be difficult to leave.

## The transition toolkit

**1. Give warnings.** Sudden stops feel unfair. Give a warning, and make it concrete:

> "Two more episodes? No, one more episode, then dinner."

> "When this level ends, it's time to stop."

**2. Use a timer.** A timer turns you from the bad guy into the messenger. *"The timer says it's time."*

**3. Make the next thing appealing.** Children transition more easily *toward* something than *away from* something. *"Screens off, then you can help me make pancakes."*

**4. Offer a landing activity.** After a screen, the brain often feels flat and restless. Have something ready: a snack, a puzzle on the table, music for a dance break, or going outside.

**5. Stay close and calm.** Be physically present when the screen goes off. Don't call it out from another room.

## A family screen agreement

Many screen battles happen because the rules change from day to day. A simple family agreement, made together when everyone is calm, removes the daily negotiation:

- When can we use screens? (For example, after homework and before dinner.)
- For how long?
- Where? (Living room, not bedrooms.)
- What happens when time is up?
- What happens if we argue about stopping? (For example, less screen time tomorrow.)

Write it down, sign it together and stick it on the fridge. When there's a disagreement, you can point to the agreement instead of raising your voice.

## What to say when the screen goes off

> "Time's up. I know it's hard to stop. You can be upset, and the screen still goes off."

> "You really wanted to finish. Let's write down where you stopped so you can start there tomorrow."

> "Screens are done. Do you want to help with dinner or play outside?"

For older children:

> "I know it's frustrating to stop in the middle. That's why we agreed on the timer. Let's stick to the plan."

## When the meltdown comes anyway

Some children will still melt down. Stay calm. You can acknowledge the feeling without giving in:

> "You're really upset. I get it. The screen is still off. I'm right here."

Then wait it out. Avoid lecturing during the meltdown. If you feel the anger rising, go back to your pause. After your child is calm, you can talk about how to make stopping easier next time.

## Transitions beyond screens

The same tools work for other hard transitions: leaving the park, stopping play for dinner, getting into the bath, leaving a friend's house.

- Warn early.
- Use a timer or a clear marker ("two more slides").
- Offer a choice about how ("hop like a bunny or walk like a robot?").
- Have something to look forward to.
- Help physically if needed, calmly.

## If it doesn't work

- **"My child melts down every single time."** Shorten screen sessions and add a predictable landing activity. Sometimes a shorter, clearer session leads to fewer battles than a longer, vaguer one.
- **"I need the screen to get things done."** That's real, and it's okay. Plan screen time for the moments you truly need it, and build the transition plan around those times.
- **"My child sneaks screens."** Keep devices in a shared space and out of bedrooms, and calmly follow through with the agreement's consequences.
- **"I'm on my phone too much myself."** Many parents are. Children notice what we do. Try a family "phone basket" during meals or the first hour after school. You'll likely feel calmer too.

# Chapter 11: Big Feelings (Theirs and Yours)

A tantrum in the supermarket. A slammed door. A child who screams "I hate you!" These moments of big feelings are some of the hardest in parenting, and among the most common triggers for yelling. When your child is out of control, it's natural to want to regain control, often by getting louder.

But a child in the middle of a big feeling can't learn, listen or reason. Their emotional brain has taken over. What they need most is a calm adult nearby, someone whose nervous system can help settle theirs.

## Why children have big feelings

Young children have strong emotions and limited skills to manage them. The parts of the brain that help with self-control, planning and calming down are still developing and will keep developing for many years. Big feelings are not bad behavior. They're a sign that a child's emotions have temporarily outgrown their skills.

Common causes include:

- Tiredness, hunger or illness
- Disappointment ("no" to a toy, a treat, a screen)
- Frustration (a task that's too hard)
- Overstimulation (noise, crowds, too much activity)
- Big changes (a new sibling, a move, a separation)
- Feeling disconnected or unseen

## Emotion coaching: a simple approach

One well-known approach to helping children with big feelings is often called **emotion coaching.** In simple terms, it means treating your child's feelings as important and helping them understand and manage them, while still holding limits on behavior.

It has five simple steps:

1. **Notice the feeling,** ideally before it becomes a full meltdown.
2. **See it as a chance to connect,** not a problem to shut down.
3. **Listen and name the feeling:** *"You're really disappointed."*
4. **Accept the feeling, limit the behavior:** *"It's okay to be angry. It's not okay to hit."*
5. **Problem-solve together,** once everyone is calm.

Naming feelings matters more than it might seem. When children hear a word for what they feel, it often helps them calm down. This idea is sometimes summed up as "name it to tame it."

## During the storm

When a child is in the middle of a tantrum, keep it simple:

- **Stay calm and close.** Your calm is contagious, even if it doesn't look like it at first.
- **Use few words.** *"You're so angry. I'm here."*
- **Keep everyone safe.** Move breakable things, move your child to a safe spot or gently hold them if they're hurting themselves or others.
- **Don't try to teach or reason yet.** That comes later.
- **Wait.** Tantrums end. Your job is to be the steady presence while it passes.

If you're in public and feel the eyes of others on you, remember: most parents watching have been exactly where you are. You might even say quietly to yourself, *"I'm helping my child through a hard moment. That's what good parents do."*

## After the storm

Once your child is calm, which might be ten minutes or two hours later, reconnect:

> "That was a really big feeling. You were so angry when we had to leave the park. Let's think about what can help next time."

Offer simple tools your child can use next time:

- Stomping feet or squeezing a pillow
- Taking "dragon breaths" (big breath in, big breath out like a dragon)
- A calm-down corner with soft things, books or a drawing pad
- Saying "I'm mad!" instead of hitting

## Your big feelings matter too

Here's the hard part: when your child has a big feeling, it often triggers yours. Their screaming can make you want to scream. Their frustration adds to your own.

This is completely normal. You will still feel angry sometimes. The goal is to be the one who calms down first. Every tool in Part One applies here: the Two-Breath Pause, stepping back, knowing your triggers, and repairing afterwards if you lose it.

It can help to remind yourself during a meltdown:

> "This is not an emergency. This is a child having a hard time. I can be calm for both of us."

## What to say: big feeling scripts

**Toddlers (2–4):**

> "Big mad! You wanted the cookie. I'm here."

**Young children (5–7):**

> "You're really frustrated that the tower fell. It's okay to be frustrated. Let's take a dragon breath together."

**Older children (8–12):**

> "I can see you're really upset. Do you want to talk, or do you need some space first? I'll be right here either way."

**For "I hate you!":**

> "You're really angry with me right now. That's okay. I still love you, even when you're angry."

This one is hard to say when you're hurt. But your child doesn't truly hate you. They're telling you, in the only words they have, that their feelings are huge.

## If it doesn't work

- **"The tantrums are getting worse."** Look at the basics first: sleep, food, routine, connection. Sometimes tantrums increase during big changes and then settle.
- **"My child is aggressive during meltdowns."** Keep everyone safe first. If aggression is frequent or causing injury, talk to your child's doctor or a family therapist.
- **"Nothing calms my child down."** Some children are more intense by temperament. Patience, consistency and support help over time. A professional can also help you understand your child's needs.
- **"I always lose it during their tantrums."** That's one of the most common struggles in this book. Focus first on your own calm, then on theirs, and repair when you slip.

# Chapter 12: When They Push Every Button

Back-talk. Eye-rolls. "You can't make me!" Ignoring you completely. Sometimes it feels like your child knows exactly which buttons to push, and pushes them all at once.

These moments can feel personal, and they often trigger a very specific kind of anger: the feeling of being disrespected. That feeling can quickly lead to a yell, or to a power struggle that leaves everyone exhausted.

## Why children push buttons

- **Testing limits.** Children need to know where the edges are. Pushing is how they find out.
- **Seeking independence.** Especially from age 2 and again in the tween years, children want more control over their lives.
- **Big feelings in disguise.** Back-talk is often frustration, embarrassment or tiredness wearing a rude mask.
- **Copying.** Children pick up tone and words from siblings, friends, screens and, sometimes, us.
- **Getting a reaction.** If button-pushing reliably gets a big reaction, it becomes interesting.

None of this means you should accept disrespect. It means you can respond without taking the bait.

## Don't take the bait

The most powerful thing you can do when your child pushes a button is to stay calm and not escalate. A big reaction often tells children that this button works. A calm, firm response tells them it doesn't.

Try:

> "That's not how we talk to each other. Try again."

> "I want to hear what you need. I'll listen when you can say it kindly."

> "You can be annoyed. You can't be rude. Let's start over."

Then wait. Don't lecture, don't threaten, don't argue. Many children will try again with a better tone if you give them a calm chance.

## Pick your battles

Not every eye-roll needs a response. Ask yourself: *Is this rude, or is this just a kid being a kid?* Sometimes the best response to a sigh or an eye-roll is no response at all. Save your energy for the things that truly matter: safety, kindness and your family's key values.

## Respond to the feeling underneath

When back-talk is really frustration, responding to the feeling can defuse it instantly:

> "Sounds like you're really frustrated about homework. Want some help?"

> "You seem upset. Did something happen at school?"

You can still deal with the rudeness later. First, look past the words to what your child is really struggling with.

## Avoid power struggles

A power struggle happens when both of you dig in and neither wants to lose. Everyone ends up losing. To avoid them:

- **Offer choices instead of commands.** *"Do you want to do homework now or after a snack?"*
- **Use "when, then."** *"When your room is tidy, then we'll go to the park."*
- **Step out of the argument.** *"I'm not going to argue about this. The answer is no."* Then stop talking.
- **Let natural consequences teach.** If your child refuses a coat, they may feel cold (when it's safe to let that happen).

## When it's really about respect

Respect is important, and you can expect it from your child. But respect is learned mostly by experiencing it. Children who are spoken to respectfully, even during conflict, learn to speak that way too. It's a slow process, but it's one of the most powerful things you can model.

This is also why yelling backfires: it teaches children that when people are upset, they get loud and harsh. Calm firmness teaches them that people can disagree, set limits and still treat each other with respect.

## Composite example: Kate and the eye-roll

Kate's ten-year-old son had started rolling his eyes and saying "Whatever" whenever she asked him to do anything. It drove her wild, and she often ended up shouting "Don't you dare roll your eyes at me!" which usually led to a slammed door.

She decided to try a different approach. The next time he rolled his eyes, she took a breath and said calmly, *"I get that you don't want to do it. I still need the dishwasher emptied before dinner."* Then she walked away. A few minutes later, she heard the dishwasher being emptied.

Later that evening, she said, *"I've noticed things have felt tense between us lately. I don't like yelling at you. Is something going on?"* Her son shrugged, but then mentioned that things were hard with a friend at school. The eye-rolls didn't disappear overnight, but they became less frequent, and the conversations became more real.

## What to say

> "Try that again, in a kind voice."

> "I'm listening. Tell me what's going on."

> "I won't argue about this. The answer is no."

> "You don't have to like it. You do have to do it."

> "I love you too much to fight with you about this."

## If it doesn't work

- **"My child is rude all the time."** Look for patterns. Constant rudeness can be a sign of stress, tiredness or feeling disconnected. More one-on-one time often helps.
- **"Calm responses feel like letting them win."** Staying calm isn't letting them win; it's refusing to play a game that nobody wins. You're still holding the limit.
- **"My child says terrible things."** Hurtful words hurt. You can say, *"That hurt my feelings. I'm going to take a break, and we'll talk later."* Then follow up when everyone is calm.
- **"I lose my temper when I feel disrespected."** This is often linked to how we were treated as children. Chapter 14 looks at this gently.

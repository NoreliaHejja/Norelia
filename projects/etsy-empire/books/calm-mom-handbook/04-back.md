# The 30-Day Calm Mom Plan

This plan turns the book into small daily steps. Each day takes about five minutes. You don't need to do the days in a perfect row; if you miss one, just pick up where you left off. Tick the box when you're done.

## Week 1: The pause and the repair

[[box]]
- [ ] **Day 1.** Practice the Two-Breath Pause three times today, once when you're calm (Chapter 1).
- [ ] **Day 2.** Choose your "early warning sign" (jaw, shoulders, voice) and notice it once.
- [ ] **Day 3.** Put a visual reminder in your most common yelling spot: a sticker, a hair tie, a note.
- [ ] **Day 4.** Write your repair sentence for your child's age and keep it on your phone (Chapter 4).
- [ ] **Day 5.** Do the HALT check at 4 p.m.: hungry, angry, lonely, tired? Fix one.
- [ ] **Day 6.** Repair one moment today, even a small one.
- [ ] **Day 7.** Write down one thing that went better this week. Just one.
[[/box]]

## Week 2: Know your triggers

[[box]]
- [ ] **Day 8.** Start your Trigger Map: note every time you raise your voice (Chapter 3).
- [ ] **Day 9.** Keep mapping. Add time of day and how hungry or tired you were.
- [ ] **Day 10.** Look at the map. Circle the time of day that shows up most.
- [ ] **Day 11.** Choose one trigger and answer the four planning questions.
- [ ] **Day 12.** Prepare your words for that trigger and say them out loud once while calm.
- [ ] **Day 13.** Try the plan for your chosen trigger.
- [ ] **Day 14.** Self-compassion check: do the three-sentence reset tonight (Chapter 13).
[[/box]]

## Week 3: Calmer days

[[box]]
- [ ] **Day 15.** Connection before correction: walk over, crouch, connect, then ask (Chapter 5).
- [ ] **Day 16.** Give ten minutes of special time to one child.
- [ ] **Day 17.** Use the feeling, limit, action sentence once (Chapter 6).
- [ ] **Day 18.** Prepare tomorrow morning tonight: clothes, bags, breakfast (Chapter 7).
- [ ] **Day 19.** Give a warning and a choice before one transition (Chapter 10).
- [ ] **Day 20.** Name one big feeling for your child: "You're really disappointed" (Chapter 11).
- [ ] **Day 21.** Pick one battle to let go of this week (Chapter 12).
[[/box]]

## Week 4: Take care of the mom

[[box]]
- [ ] **Day 22.** Eat a real snack before the hardest part of your day (Chapter 16).
- [ ] **Day 23.** Choose two micro-refills and do them today.
- [ ] **Day 24.** Agree on a tag-out word with your partner or a friend (Chapter 15).
- [ ] **Day 25.** Hand over one part of the invisible load.
- [ ] **Day 26.** Fill in the "keep, change, let go" box (Chapter 14).
- [ ] **Day 27.** Fill in your support list (Chapter 17).
- [ ] **Day 28.** Repeat the planning questions for a second trigger.
- [ ] **Day 29.** Ask your child: "What's something nice we did together this month?"
- [ ] **Day 30.** Write a kind-friend letter to yourself about how far you've come.
[[/box]]

## After 30 days

Look back at your first Trigger Map. Most mothers find fewer entries, faster repairs or both. Keep the habits that helped, drop the ones that didn't fit, and come back to this plan whenever life gets louder: a new baby, a move, a hard season at work. Going back to the basics is how you keep the progress.

# Your Fridge Card

Copy or photograph this page and stick it where you need it most.

[[box]]
**When I feel it rising**

1. **Pause.** Two slow breaths, longer out than in.
2. **Lower.** Lower your voice, lower your body, lower the volume.
3. **Name it.** "I'm getting frustrated. I'm taking a breath."
4. **One sentence.** Feeling + limit + action. "You're mad. I won't let you hit. I'm moving you to the couch."
5. **Check HALT.** Hungry? Angry? Lonely? Tired? Fix one thing.

**If I yelled anyway**

- Calm down first.
- Go back. Get low.
- "I yelled. That wasn't okay. It's my job to stay calm. I'm sorry. I love you."
- Make a plan for next time.

**Remember:** Repair matters more than perfection.
[[/box]]

# The Script Library

All the key sentences from this book, sorted by situation. Borrow them, change them, make them yours.

## The moment you feel it rising

> "I'm getting really frustrated. I'm going to take a breath before I talk."

> "I need a minute. I'm going to the kitchen and I'll be right back."

> "My voice is getting loud. Let me start again."

## Repair after yelling

**Ages 2–4:**

> "Mommy yelled. That was too loud. I'm sorry. I love you. Hug?"

**Ages 5–7:**

> "I yelled at you before. That wasn't okay. It's my job to stay calm, even when I'm upset. I'm sorry. Next time I'll take a breath first."

**Ages 8–12:**

> "I'm sorry I shouted. You didn't deserve that. I was frustrated about being late, and I took it out on you. Can we figure out a better way for mornings together?"

## Setting a calm, firm limit

> "You're really mad that it's time to go. It's okay to be mad. We're still leaving. I'll carry you or you can walk."

> "I won't let you hit. I'm going to hold your hands until you're calm."

> "The answer is no. You can be upset about it. I'm right here."

> "I'm not going to argue about this. I've decided."

## Mornings

> "Shoes, then breakfast. Which shoe goes on first?"

> "Check your picture chart. What's next?"

> "We have five minutes. Let's race the timer."

## Bedtime

> "This is the last story. Then it's lights out and cuddles."

> "I'll check on you in five minutes."

> "You can use your bedtime ticket now, or save it for later."

## Siblings

> "I see two kids who both want the same thing. That's a tough problem. What could you do?"

> "I won't let you hurt each other. You sit here, you sit there."

> "I'm not deciding who started it. I'm helping you solve it."

## Transitions and screens

> "When this episode ends, the screen goes off. Let's find a good stopping point."

> "Time's up. I know it's hard to stop. You can be upset, and the screen still goes off."

> "Screens are done. Do you want to help with dinner or go outside?"

## Big feelings

> "You're so angry. I'm here."

> "That was a big disappointment. It's okay to cry."

> "Do you want a hug or some space? I'll be right here either way."

## Back-talk and button-pushing

> "Try that again, in a kind voice."

> "You can be annoyed. You can't be rude. Let's start over."

> "You don't have to like it. You do have to do it."

> "I love you too much to fight with you about this."

## "I hate you!"

> "You're really angry with me right now. That's okay. I still love you, even when you're angry."

## To your partner

> "I'm close to losing it. Can you take over for ten minutes?"

> "Can we agree to back each other up in front of the kids and talk about disagreements later?"

## To grandparents and relatives

> "We're trying something different in our family. It would help me so much if you could do it our way with the kids."

## To yourself

> "This is a hard moment. Many parents feel this way. May I be patient with myself."

> "My child doesn't need a perfect mom. She needs a mom who comes back and repairs."

> "This is an old feeling. It's loud, but it's not in charge."

# Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal to yell at my kids?**
Many loving parents yell sometimes, especially when they are tired, stressed or alone with the load. It's common, and it can change. The goal of this book is less yelling and faster repair, not a perfect record.

**Will yelling damage my child?**
Researchers have found that harsh verbal discipline that happens often, especially shouting combined with insults or threats, is linked with more emotional and behavior problems in children, even in otherwise warm families. Occasional yelling followed by a sincere repair is a different situation. Repair teaches children that relationships can survive conflict. Focus on reducing the frequency and repairing every time.

**How long does it take to yell less?**
Many mothers notice a difference within the first week of practicing the Two-Breath Pause. Lasting change usually takes several weeks of practice, and there will be setbacks along the way. Setbacks are part of the process.

**What if I yell during the repair?**
Stop, take your breaths, and come back later. A repair only works when you're calm. "I'm still too upset to talk about it. I'll come back in ten minutes" is a perfectly good sentence.

**Should I apologize every time?**
Yes, when you've yelled at your child, a short, sincere apology helps. Keep it brief and don't turn it into a lecture about what they did wrong. Keep the limit, and own your part.

**My child doesn't react to anything unless I yell. What now?**
Children learn quickly which request is the "real" one. If calm requests have never been followed through, they get ignored. Start making the first calm request the real one: say it once, then act calmly (Chapters 5 and 6). It may get worse for a few days before it gets better.

**What if my partner yells and I don't?**
Share what's helped you, without lecturing. Offer the tag-out word and the shared-rules talk from Chapter 15. You can also repair with your child for what they experienced: "That was loud. Are you okay?"

**Is it okay to cry in front of my children?**
Yes. Showing feelings and then calming down shows children that big emotions are manageable. Keep it simple: "I'm feeling sad. I'm going to take some breaths, and I'll feel better soon."

**I'm a single parent with no one to tag out. What can I do?**
Use micro-refills (Chapter 16), safe breaks (put a small child somewhere safe and step into the next room for a minute), and a friend you can text. Look for small trades with other parents. If you're overwhelmed most days, please talk to your doctor.

**Can these tools work with a strong-willed or neurodivergent child?**
Many of them can, with adjustments. Children with ADHD, anxiety or sensory differences often need more structure, more warning before transitions and fewer words. If daily life is very hard, an assessment and specialist support can help the whole family (Chapter 17).

**What if my anger scares me?**
Please don't wait. Read Chapter 17 and reach out today. You deserve support, and so do your children.

# A Last Word

If you've read this far, you've done something many people never do: you looked honestly at a part of yourself you're not proud of, and you decided to work on it. That takes courage.

You won't get it right every day. Nobody does. On the hard days, come back to the basics: breathe, lower your voice, repair. On the good days, notice them, because they'll start to add up.

Your children don't need a perfect mother. They need you: tired, loving, learning, and always coming back.

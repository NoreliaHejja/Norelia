# Ten Hard Moments, Step by Step

The chapters in this book explain the tools. This section shows them working together in ten situations that mothers mention again and again. Each one follows the same pattern: what's going on, what to do first, what to say, and what to do afterwards. Read the ones that match your life, and skip the rest.

## 1. The supermarket meltdown

**What's going on.** Your child wanted something, you said no, and now they are on the floor in aisle five. Everyone seems to be watching. The hardest part is often the audience: you feel judged, so you want the crying to stop fast, and that pressure pushes you toward shouting or giving in.

**First.** Breathe and remind yourself that most people watching have been exactly where you are. Your job is to look after your child, not to manage strangers' opinions.

**What to say.**

> "You really wanted that. It's so hard to hear no. I'm right here."

If your child is safe, stay close and wait. If they are in the way or unsafe, move them calmly:

> "I'm going to carry you to a quiet spot. We'll come back when your body is calm."

**Afterwards.** Once your child is calm, keep it short: "That was a big feeling. Next time we'll write the treat on the list before we go." Before the next trip, plan: shop after a snack, give your child a job (holding the list, finding the bananas) and agree in advance on one small treat or none.

## 2. Fighting in the car

**What's going on.** Children are stuck side by side, bored and close enough to poke each other. You're driving, so you can't intervene physically, and the noise makes it hard to concentrate. Many parents end up shouting over their shoulder.

**First.** Safety comes before teaching. If the noise is making it hard to drive safely, pull over when you can.

**What to say.**

> "I need it quiet so I can drive safely. I'm pulling over until the car is calm."

Then wait, calmly, without lecturing. Most children settle quickly when the car actually stops.

**Afterwards.** Prepare the next trip: audiobooks or a playlist each child chooses in turn, a "car basket" of small toys, seat rotation and a simple rule like "hands in your own space." Long trips go better with planned breaks every hour or so.

## 3. Homework battles

**What's going on.** Your child is tired after school, the task feels too hard or too boring, and you're trying to cook dinner at the same time. Homework fights often mix your worry about school with their frustration and both of your tiredness.

**First.** Check the basics: has your child eaten, moved and had a break since school? A snack and fifteen minutes outside can save an hour of arguing.

**What to say.**

> "This one looks frustrating. Let's do the first question together, then you try the next one."

> "You don't have to like it. Let's set the timer for ten minutes and see how far we get."

If emotions are running high:

> "We're both getting upset. Let's take a five-minute break and come back."

**Afterwards.** Agree on a regular homework time and place. If homework causes tears most days, talk to the teacher. It may be too much, or your child may need extra support.

## 4. Dinner table standoffs

**What's going on.** You cooked, your child refuses to eat, a sibling is kicking under the table, and the meal you hoped would be calm turns into a battle.

**First.** Remember what you can control: what food is served and when. Your child decides whether to eat and how much. Many feeding specialists recommend this division of responsibility because it takes the power struggle off the table.

**What to say.**

> "You don't have to eat it. This is what's for dinner. The kitchen closes after dinner until snack time."

> "Feet on your own side, please. If kicking continues, I'll move your chair."

**Afterwards.** Serve one food your child usually eats with every meal, keep portions small and avoid bribes and battles. Try to make dinner about connection: one question each, like "What was the best part of your day?"

## 5. Getting out of the house with a baby and a toddler

**What's going on.** You need two children dressed, fed and in the car. The baby needs a diaper change at the last minute, the toddler refuses shoes, and you're already late. This is one of the most common yelling moments for parents of young children.

**First.** Lower the bar. Many families find it easier to plan for leaving fifteen minutes earlier than they need to, and to accept that some days you'll be late anyway.

**What to say.**

> "We're going to the car. Shoes on now, or shoes in my hand and you put them on in the car?"

> "I need to change the baby. You can be my helper and bring the wipes."

**Afterwards.** Prepare the night before: bag packed, clothes laid out, car seat ready. Keep a spare set of shoes and a jacket in the car. Use a picture chart for the toddler (Chapter 7).

## 6. "Mom! Mom! Mom!" while you're on the phone

**What's going on.** You need to make one call or answer one message, and your child suddenly needs you urgently, repeatedly, loudly. Children often seek attention exactly when they feel your attention go somewhere else.

**First.** Prepare before you pick up the phone, when possible.

**What to say.**

Before the call:

> "I need to make a phone call. It will take five minutes. Here's your special phone-call box. If you need me, put your hand on my arm and I'll squeeze it so you know I saw you."

During the call, if needed, mute and say calmly:

> "I see you need me. I'll be done in two minutes."

**Afterwards.** Thank your child for waiting and give them your full attention for a few minutes. The "hand on the arm" signal is widely used in families and teaches waiting without ignoring the child.

## 7. The toddler who bites or hits

**What's going on.** Young children often hit or bite when they're overwhelmed, frustrated or excited, because they don't yet have the words or control to do something else. It can feel shocking, especially if it's aimed at a baby or another child.

**First.** Stay calm and act quickly. Separate gently but firmly.

**What to say.**

> "I won't let you bite. Biting hurts."

Then turn to the hurt child first:

> "Are you okay? Let's put something cold on it."

Then, once calm, to the child who bit:

> "You were really frustrated. You can stomp your feet or say 'I'm mad!' instead."

**Afterwards.** Look for patterns: tiredness, crowds, transitions. Stay close in those moments and step in early. If biting or hitting is frequent or intense, talk to your pediatrician.

## 8. The tween who ignores you completely

**What's going on.** You asked three times. Your eleven-year-old is staring at their screen and acting as if you don't exist. It feels disrespectful, and it often triggers the urge to shout.

**First.** Connection before correction works at every age (Chapter 5). Walk over, stand where they can see you, and wait until they look up.

**What to say.**

> "I need your eyes for a moment. Thanks. The dishwasher needs emptying before dinner."

If they keep ignoring you:

> "I'm going to pause the screen until it's done. You can turn it back on afterwards."

**Afterwards.** Tweens respond well to being included in decisions. Agree together on household jobs and screen rules, and write them down. Keep up regular one-on-one time; it matters as much at twelve as it does at four.

## 9. Sickness and broken sleep

**What's going on.** Everyone is sick, or the baby is teething, and nobody has slept properly for days. Your window of tolerance is tiny, and so is your child's.

**First.** Lower every expectation you can. This is survival mode, and survival mode has different rules.

**What to say.**

To your child:

> "We're all tired and sick. Today is a cozy day. We're going to rest and watch a movie."

To yourself:

> "This is temporary. My only job today is to get everyone through it safely."

**Afterwards.** When things settle, repair any rough moments and go back to your normal routines gently. Ask for help during these weeks: a meal from a friend, a grandparent for an afternoon, your partner on night duty.

## 10. You've had a terrible day at work

**What's going on.** You walk in the door with nothing left. Your children have been waiting for you all day and want everything at once. Many mothers feel the first ten minutes at home decide the whole evening.

**First.** Give yourself a transition, even a tiny one. Sit in the car for two minutes, take three slow breaths and decide one thing: "I'm going to greet them first."

**What to say.**

> "I missed you! I've had a hard day, so I'm going to sit with you for a few minutes before I start dinner."

> "I'm feeling grumpy from work. It's not about you. I'm going to change my clothes and then I'll be ready to play."

**Afterwards.** Plan easy dinners for your hardest work days, and look at whether your partner or another adult can take the first half hour on those days. A predictable "welcome home" routine (a hug, a snack together, ten minutes of play) often prevents the evening spiral.

## What these ten moments have in common

If you look across these situations, the same few steps keep appearing:

- Look after your body first (breath, food, a pause).
- Connect with your child before you correct them.
- Say one short, calm sentence and then act.
- Repair afterwards if it went badly.
- Plan for next time while you're calm.

You don't need ten different strategies. You need a handful of tools, practiced often enough that they're there when you need them.

# A Letter Before We Begin

*It's the second week of December. The lights are up on every street. Your phone buzzes with a photo of someone else's perfect table, and a voice inside you whispers the thing you'd never say out loud: "Everyone has someone. Except me."*

*If you know that voice, this book was written for you.*

The candle in the window on this book's cover is still lit. So are you. Whatever this season has taken from you (a person, a marriage, a tradition, a picture of how life was supposed to look), it has not taken your warmth. This book will help you find it again, one evening at a time.

If you picked up this book, there is a good chance that the holidays feel a little heavy right now. Maybe you are newly single. Maybe you have been single for years and usually love it, until December arrives with its couples in matching sweaters, its family group chats, and its endless question: *"So, what are your plans?"* Maybe someone you love is gone, or far away, or simply not the person you hoped they would be. Maybe you are not sad at all, just tired of feeling like the season was designed for someone else.

Whatever brought you here, I want you to know one thing before anything else: **there is nothing wrong with you.** The holidays are loud. They come with a script, and most of us were never asked whether we wanted the part we were given. Feeling lonely, irritated, nostalgic, relieved, or all of those within one afternoon is not a failure. It is a normal human response to a season that turns the volume up on everything.

This book will not ask you to pretend. It will not tell you to "just be grateful" or to "put yourself out there" before you are ready. Instead, it will help you do three practical things:

1. **Understand what you are feeling**, so the feelings stop running the show.
2. **Plan the season on purpose**, day by day, so the hardest days do not catch you unprepared.
3. **Build a holiday that actually feels like yours**, with small rituals, real connection and a lot less pressure.

Each chapter ends with a short practice: a question to journal, a script to borrow, or a worksheet to fill in. You do not have to do them all. Pick what helps. Skip what doesn't. Come back to a page on a hard evening. This is a companion, not homework.

A gentle note: this book is meant for the ordinary heaviness that many women feel during the holidays. If what you are carrying feels bigger than that, if you are struggling to get through the day, or if you have thoughts of harming yourself, please read the chapter "When It's More Than the Holiday Blues" first and reach out to a professional or a crisis line today. You deserve real support, not just a book.

Now, make yourself something warm to drink. Let's begin.

# Chapter 1: Name What You Feel

![](ch1.jpg)

The first step is the simplest and, oddly, the one most of us skip. We go straight from *"I feel bad"* to *"What is wrong with me?"* without ever stopping to ask *"What exactly am I feeling?"*

Feelings that are named become smaller. Feelings that stay vague grow in the dark.

## The holiday feelings nobody mentions

Here are some of the emotions women describe when they spend the holidays single, separated, widowed, or simply without the picture-perfect setup. See which ones sound familiar:

- **Loneliness**: the ache of wanting someone to share the moments with.
- **Grief**: for a person, a marriage, a family tradition, or a version of life you expected to have by now.
- **Comparison**: the sting of scrolling past engagement photos and family pajamas.
- **Shame**: the sense that being alone says something bad about you. (It does not.)
- **Anger**: at an ex, at family members, at the endless questions.
- **Relief**: yes, relief. Many women feel lighter without a difficult partner or a tense family gathering, and then feel guilty about the relief.
- **Pressure**: to make the season magical for children, parents or friends, even when you are running on empty.
- **Nostalgia**: for holidays that may never have been as perfect as memory paints them.

You might feel several of these at once. That is not confusion. That is being human in December.

## Why the holidays amplify everything

Three things make this season emotionally louder than the rest of the year:

**1. The script.** Movies, ads and social media all tell the same story: a cozy home, a partner, laughing children, a big family table. When your life does not match the script, your brain registers it as a gap, even if your life is good.

**2. The calendar.** Holidays are anniversaries. They hold memories of every year before, which means they hold every loss too.

**3. The pause.** Work slows down. Routines disappear. The things that normally keep us busy and connected (colleagues, gym classes, school runs) take a break, and the quiet can feel very loud.

Understanding this does not make the feelings disappear. But it helps you stop taking them as evidence that your life is going wrong. They are a predictable reaction to a predictable season.

## The "and" practice

One of the most freeing tools for this season is a tiny word: *and.* Instead of choosing between "I am fine" and "I am falling apart," try holding both:

> I miss the way Christmas used to be, *and* I am proud of the life I am building.

> I feel lonely tonight, *and* I know this feeling will pass.

> I am relieved I am not with him this year, *and* I am sad that it ended.

You are allowed to feel more than one thing. The "and" makes room for all of you.

## Practice: Your feelings check-in

Take five minutes. Write down what you are feeling right now, as specifically as you can. Use the list above if it helps. Then finish the sentence: *"Underneath this feeling, what I really need is…"*

Most feelings point to a need: rest, connection, reassurance, a change of scene, a good cry. Once you know the need, you can start meeting it. That is what the rest of this book is about.

# Chapter 2: Step Out of the Comparison Trap

There is a particular kind of loneliness that only arrives through a screen. You are fine, genuinely fine, until you see a friend's photo of a candlelit dinner for two, or a cousin's family of five in matching reindeer sweaters. Suddenly your quiet evening feels like proof that you are missing out.

## What you are actually comparing

When you compare your life to someone else's holiday photo, you are comparing your whole, unfiltered reality, including the dishes in the sink and the thoughts at 2 a.m., to one carefully chosen second of theirs. You do not see the argument in the car on the way there. You do not see the partner who scrolled their phone through dinner, or the mother who cried in the bathroom because the turkey burned.

This is not to say that other people are secretly miserable. Many of them are happy, and that is lovely. The point is simply this: **a photo is not a life.** It is a moment, edited and shared. Your life deserves to be judged by how it feels from the inside, not by how it would look on someone else's feed.

## A gentle digital plan for December

You do not need to delete every app. But you might want to decide, ahead of time, how and when you will use them. Here are a few ideas women have found helpful:

- **Mute, don't unfollow.** Most platforms let you quietly mute accounts for 30 days. Nobody is notified. Use it generously.
- **Set "no-scroll windows."** For example, no social media after 9 p.m., or not on Christmas Eve and Christmas Day.
- **Move the apps.** Put social apps in a folder on the last screen of your phone. A few extra taps are often enough to break the habit.
- **Replace, don't just remove.** When you reach for your phone, have something else ready: an audiobook, a crossword, a podcast, a friend to text directly.
- **Post for yourself, if you post at all.** If you share your holiday, share what you enjoyed, not what you think will impress.

## Your own highlight reel

Here is a small, surprisingly powerful exercise. For the next seven days, write down one good moment from each day. Not big moments. Small ones: the first sip of coffee, a funny message from a friend, the smell of oranges, finishing a chapter of a book, a stranger holding a door.

By the end of the week, you will have your own highlight reel, and it will be real.

## Practice: The comparison question

The next time you feel the sting of comparison, ask yourself: *"What is this photo showing me that I want?"* Sometimes the answer is "a partner." But often it is something smaller and more reachable: warmth, company, a reason to dress up, a beautiful table, a tradition. Write down three things you noticed you wanted this week, and one small way you could give yourself a version of each.

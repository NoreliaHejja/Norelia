# Chapter 9: The First Holidays After a Breakup or Divorce

The first holiday season after a relationship ends is its own kind of hard. Everything is a first: the first Christmas card signed with only your name, the first time decorating alone, the first family gathering where people tiptoe around the empty chair. Even when the ending was the right decision, even when you chose it, the firsts can catch you off guard.

## Expect the "ambush moments"

Grief after a breakup rarely arrives on schedule. It tends to ambush you in ordinary places: a song in the supermarket, the box of ornaments you bought together, the restaurant where you always had the holiday lunch, the smell of a particular cologne in a crowded shop.

You cannot avoid every ambush moment, but you can prepare for the predictable ones:

- **The decorations.** Go through them before you decorate. Keep what you love, give away or store what hurts. You are allowed to start a completely new collection.
- **Shared traditions.** List the traditions you shared. For each one, decide: keep it, change it, or pause it for this year.
- **Mutual friends and family.** Expect that some invitations will change. Some people will choose sides, some will not know what to do. Give them, and yourself, a little grace.
- **Social media.** Seriously consider muting your ex and their close circle for the season. Seeing their holiday photos will not help you heal.

## If you share children

The first holiday season of co-parenting can be the most painful of all. A few principles help:

1. **Agree on the schedule early,** ideally in writing, so there are no last-minute surprises.
2. **Keep the handovers calm and short.** Children notice tension more than words.
3. **Don't compete on gifts.** Children remember time and warmth far more than price tags.
4. **Let them love the other parent's Christmas too.** Saying "I hope you have a wonderful time at Dad's" is a gift to them, even if it costs you something.
5. **Plan your child-free hours with special care.** These are your hardest hours. Do not leave them empty.

## When you still love them

Sometimes the hardest part isn't anger; it's missing them. You may find yourself wanting to send a "Merry Christmas" message, or wondering whether the holidays might soften things. Before you reach out, ask yourself three questions:

- What am I hoping will happen if I send this?
- How will I feel if they don't reply, or reply coldly?
- Will I feel proud of this message in a week?

If the answers make you hesitate, write the message in your journal instead. Read it again on January 2nd. You can still send it then, if you truly want to. Most women find they don't.

# Chapter 10: Holidays After a Loss

For some women, being alone during the holidays is not about relationships at all. It is about someone who died: a parent, a partner, a child, a close friend. Grief and the holidays are deeply tangled, because the season is built on memory, family and tradition.

There is no "right" way to grieve during the holidays. Some people want to keep every tradition exactly the same. Others can't bear to. Both are valid. You can also change your mind halfway through December.

## Make room for the person who is missing

Many people find comfort in actively including their loved one in the season, rather than trying not to think about them:

- Light a candle for them on a particular evening.
- Cook their signature dish, or ask someone to share their recipe.
- Hang an ornament in their memory.
- Write them a letter and read it aloud, or keep it.
- Donate to a cause they cared about.
- Tell stories about them at a gathering: "Do you remember when she…?"

You may worry that mentioning them will make others sad. Often, the opposite is true. People who loved them may be waiting for permission to say their name.

## Tell people what you need

Friends and family often don't know how to help a grieving person during the holidays. They may avoid the subject entirely or try too hard to cheer you up. It helps enormously to be specific:

- "I'd love it if you would mention Mom at dinner. It helps me to hear her name."
- "I might need to leave early. Please don't worry if I do."
- "I don't want to talk about it today, but I'd love a hug."
- "Could you call me on the 26th? That's the anniversary and I'd like to hear a friendly voice."

## Grief waves

Grief does not move in a straight line. It comes in waves, sometimes gentle, sometimes overwhelming. During a wave:

1. Find somewhere you can be alone for a few minutes, if possible.
2. Breathe slowly: in for four counts, out for six.
3. Let the tears come, if they come. They are not a sign of going backwards.
4. When the wave passes, do one small grounding thing: drink a glass of water, wash your face, step outside.

# Chapter 11: Talk to Yourself Like a Friend

Notice the way you speak to yourself during a hard evening. For many women, the inner voice is far harsher than anything they would ever say to a friend:

*"Of course you're alone. Who would want to spend Christmas with you?"*
*"Everyone else has their life together."*
*"You should be over this by now."*

If a friend said any of those things to you, you would probably stop calling them. Yet many of us live with that voice every day.

## The friend test

The next time you hear that inner critic, try this simple test. Ask: *"What would I say to my best friend if she said this about herself?"* Then say that to yourself, in the same tone you'd use with her.

> Critic: "You're pathetic for crying over this."
> Friend: "Of course you're crying. You loved him, and it's Christmas. That's a lot. Let's get you a cup of tea."

> Critic: "Everyone else has a family to go to."
> Friend: "Lots of people are spending this time in different ways. You're building something new. That takes courage."

It may feel awkward at first. That's normal. With practice, the friendlier voice gets louder.

## Three steps of self-compassion

Psychologists who study self-compassion often describe it in three parts. You can practice all three in a single minute:

1. **Notice:** "This is a hard moment." (Name it, without judgment.)
2. **Remember you're not alone:** "Many women feel this way during the holidays." (Because they do.)
3. **Offer kindness:** "May I be gentle with myself tonight." (Or put a hand on your heart, or wrap yourself in a blanket.)

## Lower the bar on purpose

The holidays come with impossible standards. You're allowed to lower them. You don't have to decorate. You don't have to send cards. You don't have to bake, host, attend every party or post a single photo. You don't have to be cheerful. You only have to take care of yourself as well as you can, one day at a time.

## Practice: A letter from your kindest friend

Write a short letter to yourself, from the point of view of the kindest friend you can imagine. What does she see in you? What would she want you to know this December?

# Chapter 12: Traveling Solo for the Holidays

For some women, the best way to spend the holidays is somewhere else entirely. A solo trip can turn a season that feels heavy into an adventure, and it removes many of the usual pressures: no family table, no couples' parties, no reminders of how things "should" be.

## Is a solo trip right for you?

A holiday trip alone can be wonderful if:

- You enjoy your own company, or want to learn to.
- You find your usual surroundings full of painful reminders.
- You'd like something to look forward to and talk about.
- Your budget allows it without causing stress in January.

It may be less helpful if you're feeling very low and isolated. In that case, a trip with a friend, or a group trip, might feel safer.

## Ideas for solo holiday travel

- **A Christmas market city:** many European cities come alive in December with lights, music and food stalls, and it's easy to feel part of the crowd.
- **A wellness retreat:** yoga, hiking, spa or meditation retreats often run special holiday programs, and many guests are traveling alone.
- **Somewhere warm:** a beach can make Christmas feel like an entirely different holiday.
- **A cozy cabin:** books, walks, a fireplace and complete quiet.
- **A group tour:** a ready-made community for the week, with no planning required.

## Staying safe and connected

- Share your itinerary with someone at home and check in daily.
- Choose well-reviewed accommodation in central, well-lit areas.
- Plan the big days (Christmas Eve, Christmas Day) in advance: a special dinner reservation, a concert, a tour.
- Bring a journal. Solo travel is a wonderful time for reflection.

# Chapter 13: Dating During the Holidays (If You Want To)

Let's be clear: you do not need to date to have a good holiday season. Being single is not a problem to be solved before December 25th. But if you *want* to meet someone, the holidays can be a surprisingly good time for it, as long as you go in with clear eyes.

## Watch out for "cuffing season" pressure

Many people feel a stronger urge to pair up when the weather turns cold and the holidays approach. That's natural. It can also lead to rushing into something just to avoid being alone at Christmas. Ask yourself honestly: *"Would I be interested in this person in June?"*

## Low-pressure ways to meet people

- Holiday events: markets, concerts, charity runs, community events
- Volunteering, which is also a wonderful way to see someone's character
- Friends' parties: tell a couple of trusted friends you're open to meeting someone
- Classes and clubs that continue into the new year
- Dating apps, with clear boundaries about time and energy

## Keep your peace

If you date during the season, a few gentle boundaries help:

- Don't introduce someone new to your family at the holidays unless you truly want to.
- Keep your own plans and traditions. Don't drop them for someone you met three weeks ago.
- Notice how you feel after a date: calmer, or more anxious? That tells you a lot.
- Remember that a disappointing date is just information, not a verdict on your worth.

## If you don't want to date

That's a complete answer. Some seasons are for healing, for friendship, for getting to know yourself again. "I'm not dating right now" is a full sentence, to others and to yourself.

# Chapter 14: Stories From the Season

The women in this chapter are composites, drawn from common experiences rather than real individuals. You may recognize a little of yourself in one of them.

## Anna, 41: "I thought I'd hate it."

Anna's divorce was finalized in October. Her ex had the children for Christmas Eve and Christmas morning that first year, and she dreaded it for weeks. Instead of leaving the time empty, she made a plan: a 10 a.m. swim, a late breakfast with a friend who was also on her own, an afternoon wrapping presents for the children's "second Christmas" on the 26th, and an early night with a new novel. "It wasn't magical," she says, "but it was mine. And when the kids came home, we had pancakes and opened presents in our pajamas, and it felt just as special."

*What helped:* anchors for every part of the day, and moving the family celebration instead of mourning the date.

## Maria, 34: "The question."

Every year, Maria's aunt asked about her love life, loudly, at the table. This year Maria decided her answer in advance and practiced it in the car: *"Not right now, and I'm really happy. Now, tell me how you made this incredible pie."* Her aunt laughed and launched into the recipe. "I couldn't believe it was that easy," Maria says. "I'd spent years dreading it."

*What helped:* deciding on a script and redirecting with a genuine question.

## Helen, 58: "The first year without him."

Helen's husband died in the spring. She couldn't face the big family Christmas, so she asked her daughter to host a small lunch instead, and they each brought one of his favorite dishes. At dessert they told stories about him, and for the first time in months she laughed. "I thought remembering him would make it harder," she says. "It made it lighter."

*What helped:* including the person who was missing, and telling family what she needed.

## Jess, 29: "Too much scrolling."

Jess realized she spent every evening in December scrolling engagement announcements and feeling worse. She muted a dozen accounts, put her phone in the kitchen at 9 p.m., and joined a winter running club. "I'm not saying I didn't feel lonely sometimes," she says, "but I felt like I was living my own life again instead of watching everyone else's."

*What helped:* a simple digital plan and replacing scrolling with something social.

# Chapter 15: A 7-Day Reset for January

January can feel like a hangover from the holidays: grey days, tight budgets, and a sudden drop in social plans. For women who found December hard, the first week of January is a perfect time for a gentle reset.

**Day 1: Rest.** Sleep in if you can. Spend the day doing as little as possible, on purpose.

**Day 2: Clear.** Take down the decorations. Open the windows. Clean one room thoroughly.

**Day 3: Move.** Choose a type of movement you might enjoy this year and try it once: a class, a long walk, a swim, a dance video at home.

**Day 4: Plan something to look forward to.** A weekend away in spring, a concert, a course, a visit with a friend. Put it in the calendar.

**Day 5: Connect.** Arrange one get-together for January. Winter is long; don't wait for someone else to organize it.

**Day 6: Money check.** Look at what you spent in December, without judgment. Make a simple plan to recover, if needed.

**Day 7: Choose your theme.** Pick one word for the year ahead: *steady, brave, home, joy, growth, ease*. Write it where you'll see it every day.

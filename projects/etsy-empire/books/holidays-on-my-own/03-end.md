# Chapter 16: Money and Gifts on Your Terms

Holiday spending carries an emotional weight that's easy to overlook. For single women, it often comes with extra layers: managing on one income, buying for nieces, nephews, friends and colleagues, feeling obliged to match what couples spend, or overspending to fill an emotional gap.

## Decide your number first

Before you buy anything, decide on a total holiday budget, one you can pay off by the end of January at the latest. Then divide it:

[[box]]
**My total holiday budget:**
[[lines:1]]
**Gifts:** ________ **Food & hosting:** ________ **Outings & travel:** ________
**Something just for me:** ________ **Charity/giving:** ________
[[/box]]

Notice that last-but-one line. Many women spend the whole season giving and nothing on themselves. A small, planned gift to yourself, a book, a candle, a massage, a nice bottle of something, is not selfish. It's a way of telling yourself that you count too.

## Gifts that cost less and mean more

- **Time gifts:** an afternoon of babysitting, a homemade dinner, a day trip together.
- **Homemade:** cookies in a pretty jar, a playlist, a printed photo, a handwritten letter.
- **Group gifts:** suggest a Secret Santa among friends or family to reduce the number of presents.
- **Experiences:** cinema vouchers, a class, a museum pass.

## It's okay to say no

If a gift exchange, a pricey night out, or an expensive trip doesn't fit your budget, you can say so kindly and clearly:

- "I'm keeping things simple this year. I'd love to just get together for coffee."
- "I'm sitting this one out, but I'll join you for the walk on Sunday."

Real friends will understand. And the relief of not starting January in debt is a gift in itself.

## Avoid the late-night "retail therapy" trap

Online shopping at midnight, after a hard evening, is one of the easiest ways to overspend. If you notice the pattern: delete saved card details, unsubscribe from sale emails, and put items in your basket but wait 24 hours before buying. Most of the urge passes.

# Chapter 17: When It's More Than the Holiday Blues

Most of what this book describes is the ordinary heaviness of a demanding season. It hurts, but it passes. Sometimes, though, what we feel is bigger, deeper, or longer-lasting than the holiday blues. It's important to know the difference, and to know that help is available.

## Signs it's time to talk to someone

Consider reaching out to your doctor or a mental health professional if, for two weeks or more, you notice:

- Feeling low, empty or hopeless most of the day, most days
- Losing interest in things you usually enjoy
- Big changes in sleep or appetite
- Difficulty concentrating or making simple decisions
- Feeling worthless, or excessively guilty
- Withdrawing from everyone
- Relying on alcohol or other substances to cope

These can be signs of depression, which is common, real and treatable. Getting help is a sign of strength, not weakness.

## If you are in crisis

If you have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, please reach out right now:

- **United States:** call or text **988** (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline)
- **United Kingdom & Ireland:** call **Samaritans at 116 123**
- **Elsewhere:** call your local emergency number, or search for your country's crisis line
- If you are in immediate danger, call emergency services

You do not have to explain everything perfectly. You only need to say: *"I'm having a really hard time and I need to talk to someone."*

Being on your own for the holidays is not a sign that something is missing from you. It's simply a chapter of a life, one that can hold peace, freedom, friendship, creativity, rest and joy, alongside the harder feelings.

# Bonus: The Scripts Library

Sometimes the hardest part is finding the words. Borrow these exactly as they are, or change them to sound like you.

## Reaching out when you feel lonely
- "Hey, I'm having a quiet evening and thought of you. Free for a call this week?"
- "I'm on my own on the 25th this year. Any chance you'd like to go for a walk that morning?"
- "This season is harder than I expected. I don't need advice, I'd just love to hear your voice."

## Inviting people over
- "I'm doing a low-key 'leftovers and movies' night on the 27th. Pajamas encouraged. Come?"
- "Cookie swap at mine on Saturday. Bring one batch, leave with six kinds."
- "New Year's Day brunch, 11 a.m., no hangover judgment. You in?"

## Declining kindly
- "Thank you so much for thinking of me. I'm keeping this year quiet, but let's get together in January."
- "I can't make the whole evening, but I'd love to stop by for an hour."
- "I'm sitting out the gift exchange this year. I'll bring the dessert instead!"

## Asking for support
- "Could you check in with me on Christmas Eve? Just a quick text would mean a lot."
- "I'd love it if we could talk about Dad at dinner. I don't want to pretend he isn't missing."
- "I might need to step out for a bit during the party. If I do, I'm fine, I just need air."

## Handling comments about being single
- "I'm really happy with my life right now. How's work going for you?"
- "When there's news, you'll be the first to know!" (Then change the subject.)
- "I'd rather not talk about dating today."

## With an ex, about the children
- "Can we confirm the holiday schedule by the 1st, in writing, so the kids know what to expect?"
- "I'll have them ready at 10 on the 24th. Please text if anything changes."
- "I hope you all have a lovely time. They're excited to see you."

# Chapter 9: Systems That Think for You

A lot of the invisible load is the same decisions, made again and again. What's for dinner. What do we need from the shop. Who's picking up on Thursday. Every one of those questions takes a little energy, and you might answer dozens of them a day.

A simple system makes the decision once and then repeats it, so nobody has to carry it in their head.

## The weekly look-ahead (15 minutes)

Pick a fixed time each week, for example Sunday evening. Sit down with your partner (or alone, or with older kids) and look at the coming week together:

1. **Calendar:** appointments, activities, work trips, school events. Who drives where?
2. **Meals:** a rough plan for dinners (see below).
3. **Shopping:** what's needed for those meals and for the house.
4. **Special things:** birthdays, forms, payments, anything unusual.
5. **Each person's areas:** each owner says what their jobs need this week.

That's it. Fifteen minutes on Sunday can save you an hour of mental juggling every day. It also makes the load visible to everyone, every week.

## One shared calendar

If the family schedule lives only in your head or your phone, you'll always be the one asked. Use one shared calendar everyone can see: a phone calendar shared between adults, or a paper calendar on the fridge for kids. The rule: if it's not on the calendar, it's not happening. Everyone adds their own events.

## Meals on repeat

Meal planning is one of the biggest daily drains. Make it smaller:

- **Theme nights:** Monday pasta, Tuesday tacos, Wednesday soup, Thursday leftovers, Friday pizza, Saturday someone else cooks, Sunday roast or eggs. Within a theme, you only choose the details.
- **A family top 20:** write down twenty meals everyone will eat. When you plan, pick from the list.
- **A two-week rotation:** plan fourteen dinners once and repeat them. Most families don't mind.
- **Cook once, eat twice:** double the recipe and freeze half.
- **A standing grocery list:** keep a master list of what you buy every week. Ordering becomes ticking boxes.

## A family command center

Choose one spot (a corner of the kitchen, a wall by the door) for:

- the calendar,
- a tray or folder for school papers and forms that need action,
- a hook or basket for each child's bag,
- a place for keys, chargers and the things everyone always asks you for.

When someone asks "Where's my...?", the answer becomes "Check the command center," not you.

## Automate what you can

- Set up automatic payments for regular bills.
- Use subscriptions for things you buy every month (nappies, pet food, cleaning supplies), if it fits your budget.
- Put recurring reminders in the shared calendar: car service every year, dentist every six months, school photo day.
- Keep a "birthday box" with a few gifts and cards ready for children's parties.

## Simplify before you organize

Before building a system, ask: does this need to happen at all? Fewer activities, fewer toys to tidy, fewer clothes to wash, fewer events in December. A simpler life needs less managing.

**Practice:** hold your first weekly look-ahead this Sunday. Put it on the calendar as a repeating event.

**If it doesn't work:** if your partner won't join the look-ahead, do it alone for now, but share the result (a photo of the week's plan in a family chat). Visibility still helps.

# Chapter 10: Good Enough Is the Standard

This chapter is the uncomfortable one. Sometimes we hold on to parts of the load because we care how things are done. The towels folded in thirds. The lunch with a vegetable in it. The present that's just right.

Caring is not a fault. But when only one person's standard counts, only one person can do the job. And then you're back to carrying it.

## Signs you might be holding on

- You redo jobs after someone else has done them.
- You give instructions while a job is being done.
- You say, "It's easier if I do it myself."
- You feel anxious when someone else is in charge of something important.
- You're praised as the one who "does it all," and part of you likes that.

Some researchers call this *maternal gatekeeping*, but you don't need the label. What matters is noticing when your standard is keeping the load on your shoulders.

## Three questions before you step in

When you feel the urge to fix or redo something, ask:

1. **Is anyone unsafe or unwell?** If yes, step in. Car seats, medicines, allergies: some things need to be right.
2. **Will this matter in a week?** A mismatched outfit, a messy sandwich, a birthday card bought at the petrol station probably won't.
3. **Is this my standard or a real need?** If it's your preference, can you let it go, or agree on a "done" that you can both live with?

## Agree on "done," then let go

For each shared job, agree on a simple definition of done (Chapter 6). Then the method belongs to the person doing it. If the kitchen is clean by 9 p.m., it doesn't matter whether it was cleaned in your order.

What to say to yourself, when it's hard:

> "Done is better than my way. I'm trading perfection for rest."

What to say to your partner, when you slip and start correcting:

> "Sorry, I'm doing it again. It's your job, your way."

## Lowering the bar on purpose

You can also choose a lower standard for yourself in certain seasons of life:

- Store-bought birthday cake.
- Fewer, simpler holiday traditions.
- Paper plates on busy nights.
- Clean enough, not spotless.
- Kids' outfits they chose themselves.

None of these make you a lesser mother. They make you a mother with some energy left.

**Practice:** this week, choose one thing you'll deliberately do "good enough." Notice what happens. (Usually: nothing bad.)

**If it doesn't work:** if letting go makes you very anxious, that's worth being gentle with. Anxiety often drives the need for control. Self-compassion helps: notice the feeling, remind yourself many mothers feel it, and treat yourself as you'd treat a tired friend. If anxiety is strong and constant, a doctor or therapist can help.

# Chapter 11: Rest Without Earning It

![](ch4.jpg)

Many women carrying the load have a hidden rule: *I can rest when everything's done.* But everything is never done. There's always one more load of laundry, one more email. So rest never comes.

Rest isn't a reward for finishing the list. It's what lets you keep going.

## Why rest feels so hard

- When you stop, your mind fills with the list.
- If you rest while others are busy, you feel guilty.
- If you rest while others are not, nothing gets done.
- You've forgotten what you even like to do.

## Protected time

Ask for, and give, protected time: a regular block each week when you are off duty. Not "off unless something comes up." Off.

> "I'd like every Saturday morning from 9 to 12 to be my time. I'll leave the house or go to the bedroom, and you're in charge of everything, including the kids' questions. Could you take Sunday afternoons for yourself the same way?"

Off duty means:

- You don't answer "Where's my...?" questions.
- You don't plan the next meal.
- You don't check in by phone (unless there's a real emergency).

The first few times may feel strange. Many women spend their first free hour doing chores. That's normal. Keep practicing.

## Small daily rests

Not every rest needs a free morning. Try a few small ones:

- Ten minutes of tea and a window before the kids wake up.
- A short walk alone after dinner, while someone else clears up.
- A bath with the door locked.
- Headphones and one song, eyes closed.
- Going to bed at the same time as the kids once a week.

## Find yourself again

The invisible load can crowd out the person you were. Make a short list called *Things I used to love*. Reading on the sofa, painting, swimming, dancing, seeing a friend, a long phone call with your sister, a class. Choose one, and put it on the shared calendar for the next month. That calendar entry is just as real as a dentist appointment.

## Self-compassion, not self-criticism

Kristin Neff's work on self-compassion offers a simple practice for hard moments. When you're overwhelmed:

1. **Notice:** "This is really hard right now."
2. **Remember you're not alone:** "Many mothers feel exactly like this."
3. **Be kind:** put a hand on your chest and say something you'd say to a friend. "You're doing so much. It's okay to rest."

It feels awkward at first. It works better than another round of "I should be doing more."

**Practice:** book your first protected time this week. Write it on the calendar, in pen.

**If it doesn't work:** if you can't get a full block of time, start with thirty minutes. If nobody else can take over, swap with another parent, use a sitter for one hour, or ask a relative. You're allowed to need help.

# Chapter 12: If You're Carrying It Alone

If you're a single mother, or your partner is away, ill or not willing to share, much of this book may have felt like it was for someone else. The truth is, you carry the full invisible load with no one to hand it to. That's a lot, and it deserves to be said out loud.

You can't share it with a partner. But you can still make it smaller.

## Shrink before you share

Everything in Chapters 9 and 10 matters even more for you:

- Simple, repeating meals.
- One calendar, one command center.
- Fewer activities and commitments.
- A lower bar, chosen on purpose, without guilt.
- Automatic payments and reminders wherever possible.

## Give kids real ownership

Children of single parents can take on age-appropriate jobs (Chapter 8) and often feel proud of being part of a small team. Be careful not to hand them adult worries (money, conflict with their other parent), but chores, packing their own bags and helping with dinner are healthy.

## Build a village, piece by piece

You don't need a big support network. Start with a few small trades:

- A carpool with one other family.
- A friend who takes your kids one afternoon a month, and you take hers.
- A grandparent or neighbor for one regular evening.
- A shared meal night with another single parent once a week.

What to say when asking:

> "I'm trying to make my weeks a bit more manageable. Would you be up for a swap? I'll take the kids on Wednesday after school, and you take them on Fridays."

Most people like being asked for something specific.

## When your co-parent is involved

If your children's other parent shares custody, some of the load can still be shared: medical appointments, school communication, clothes and supplies at each house. A shared calendar or a co-parenting app can help keep things factual and calm. Keep requests short and practical, and focus on the children's needs. (If you're rebuilding after a divorce, *The Woman Who Started Over*, another book in this series, covers the early months in more detail.)

## Rest is not optional for you

Because there's nobody to take over, rest has to be planned on purpose. Use school hours, sleepovers, a sitter for a few hours, or the time your children are with their other parent. Don't spend all of it on chores. Put at least one thing on the list just for you.

**Practice:** write down two people you could ask for one small swap. Ask one of them this week.

**If it doesn't work:** if you have no one nearby, look for local parent groups, community centers, places of worship or online groups for single parents in your area. Many towns have family support services that can help with childcare or practical support. And if the load is crushing you, please talk to your doctor. You matter too.

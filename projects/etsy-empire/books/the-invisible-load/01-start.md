# Before You Begin

It's 10:40 at night. The kitchen is finally quiet. You sit down, and your mind starts reading out a list nobody asked it to keep: the permission slip, the dentist, the birthday party on Saturday that still needs a present, the shoes your son outgrew last week, the milk, the email from the teacher you haven't answered, your mother's medication, the car insurance.

Nobody else in the house is thinking about any of it. They're asleep, or scrolling, or watching something. And if you said it all out loud, you'd probably hear, "Why didn't you just ask me?"

This book is for the woman who remembers. The one who knows where the spare charger is, when the library books are due and which child won't eat anything green this month. If you're tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix, there's a good chance you're carrying what many people now call the invisible load, or the mental load: the thinking, planning, noticing and remembering that keeps a family running.

You don't need a big new system to start feeling lighter. You need a few honest conversations, a handful of simple tools, and permission to stop holding everything in your head. That's what the next 30 days are for.

A few promises before we start:

- You won't be asked to do more. Every chapter is about putting something down.
- You don't need a perfect partner, or any partner. Chapter 12 is for women carrying this alone.
- You can start tonight. Chapter 1 takes about twenty minutes and a pen.

Read it in order if you can. If you're short on time, read Chapter 1, then jump to the 30-Day Reset at the back and come back to the chapters as each day points you to them.

A gentle note: this is a self-help book, not medical or psychological advice. If you feel low most days, can't sleep, or have thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to your doctor or a crisis line today. Chapter 3 tells you more.

# Chapter 1: Tonight, Empty Your Head

![](ch1.jpg)

Your brain is not a filing cabinet. It's very good at noticing what needs doing, and very bad at storing it. When you keep a hundred small tasks in your head, part of your attention stays on them all day, like tabs left open on a computer. That background hum is a big part of why you feel tired even on days when you "didn't do much."

So the first step is simple, and you can do it tonight: get everything out of your head and onto paper.

## The brain dump

Take a notebook or a few sheets of paper. Set a timer for fifteen minutes. Write down every single thing you're keeping track of. Don't sort it, don't judge it, don't make it neat. Big and small go on the same list.

If you get stuck, walk through these prompts:

- **The house:** repairs, cleaning, laundry, groceries, bills, things that are running out.
- **The kids:** school forms, appointments, clothes that don't fit, activities, homework, friendships you're worried about, birthdays they're invited to.
- **Food:** what's for dinner this week, lunches, snacks, who doesn't eat what.
- **Calendar:** holidays, travel, visits, deadlines, renewals.
- **Other people:** your parents, in-laws, friends, gifts, cards, thank-you notes.
- **Money:** subscriptions, savings, the thing you meant to cancel.
- **You:** the doctor's appointment you keep postponing, the haircut, the friend you miss.

Most women write between sixty and a hundred and fifty items the first time. If your list is long, that's not a sign you're disorganized. It's a picture of how much you've been holding.

## Three marks

Now read through the list once and give each item one of three marks:

- **D** for *drop*: it doesn't really need to happen, or not this month.
- **S** for *share*: someone else in the family could own it.
- **K** for *keep*: it's yours for now.

Be honest with the D's. The handmade class gift, the perfectly sorted toy bins, the third birthday party this month. You're allowed to decide that something isn't worth your energy right now.

## One handover tonight

Pick one S item that's small, repeated and clear. Taking out the recycling on Tuesday nights. Packing the gym bag. Ordering the dog food. Choose one, and tonight or tomorrow morning, hand it over completely. Not "can you help me with," but "this is yours now."

**What to say:**

> "I've realized I'm keeping track of a lot of things in my head, and it's wearing me out. Could you take over the recycling completely? That means remembering it's Tuesday, taking it out and noticing when we need new bags. I won't remind you."

Then, and this is the hard part, don't remind. If it gets missed once, the world won't end. Chapter 6 explains why the reminding is the part that keeps you tired.

## Keep the list somewhere outside your head

Your brain dump is the start of a family inventory (Chapter 4). For now, put it somewhere you'll see it: a notebook on the counter, a note on your phone, a sheet on the fridge. When something new pops into your head during the day, add it to the list instead of carrying it.

Try it for one evening and notice how you feel when you go to bed. Many women say it's the first time in months their mind goes quiet.

**If it doesn't work:** if writing the list makes you feel worse, panicked by how long it is, stop at ten minutes and only mark the first twenty items. You don't need to solve it all tonight. Seeing it is the first step, and you've just taken it.

# Chapter 2: What the Invisible Load Really Is

Most couples don't argue about the dishes. They argue about who noticed the dishes needed doing.

The invisible load isn't the tasks themselves. It's everything that happens before and after them: knowing a task exists, thinking about when and how it should be done, making sure it actually happened. Researchers sometimes call this *cognitive labor*. In a well-known study published in 2019, sociologist Allison Daminger interviewed couples about how they ran their homes and described this work in four parts:

1. **Anticipating:** noticing a need before it becomes a problem. *The baby will need bigger clothes soon. We're almost out of dishwasher tablets. Grandma's birthday is next week.*
2. **Identifying options:** looking for ways to meet the need. *Which summer camps are near us? Where can we get school shoes on Saturday?*
3. **Deciding:** choosing one of those options.
4. **Monitoring:** checking that it happened and that it worked. *Did the form get signed? Did he take his medicine? Is the camp booked?*

In that study, the women did more of this work overall, and especially more of the anticipating and monitoring. Deciding was shared more evenly. That pattern matches what many women describe: "He'll happily choose which camp, once I've found three options, checked the dates and put the forms in front of him. And then I'm the one who remembers to pay."

## Why it stays invisible

There are a few reasons the load is so hard to see, even for loving, hardworking partners:

- **It happens in your head.** Nobody sees you think about the dentist while you're driving.
- **When it works, nothing happens.** The house doesn't run out of toilet paper. The birthday present appears. Success looks like nothing at all.
- **It happens in tiny pieces.** Two minutes here, thirty seconds there. It never feels like "a job" on its own, so it's never counted as one.
- **It was learned early.** Many of us grew up watching our own mothers carry it, and it slid onto our shoulders without a decision.

None of this means your partner is a bad person. Often he (or she) truly doesn't see it, the same way you don't notice the hum of the fridge until it stops. That's why the work in this book starts with making the load visible, not with blame.

## The "helping" trap

Listen to how people talk about it: "He helps a lot." "She's lucky, he helps with the kids." Helping means the job belongs to someone else, and you're lending a hand. If your partner helps with the laundry, the laundry is still yours. You're still the one who notices the basket is full, knows which things can't go in the dryer and remembers that the school uniform has to be clean by Monday.

The goal of this book is not more help. It's fewer things that belong only to you.

## How to explain it in one minute

When you want to describe the invisible load to someone who doesn't feel it, an example works better than a theory:

> "Think about a child's birthday party we're invited to. Before you can buy the present, someone has to see the invitation, put it on the calendar, ask what the child likes, decide on a budget, buy it, wrap it, write the card and remember to take it on the day. Buying the present is the part people see. The other eight steps are the invisible part. I'm doing those for almost everything in our family, and I'm running out of room."

**Practice:** take three tasks from your brain dump and write out every hidden step behind them, the way the birthday example does. Keep this page. You'll use it in Chapter 5.

**If it doesn't work:** if you read this and think, "But I like being in control of these things," that's worth noticing too. Some of the load is genuinely yours by choice, and that's fine. The question is whether you chose it, or whether it just landed on you. Chapter 10 looks at the parts we hold on to.

# Chapter 3: Quiet Burnout, and How to Spot It

Burnout rarely arrives with a crash. For mothers, it often arrives as a slow fade: you stop enjoying things, snap more easily and feel like you're watching your life from a little distance.

Researchers who study *parental burnout* (Isabelle Roskam and Moïra Mikolajczak in Belgium are two of the best known) describe it with a few core signs:

- **Deep exhaustion** linked to parenting and running the home, not just being busy.
- **Emotional distance** from your children: going through the motions, doing what's needed but feeling flat.
- **Losing pleasure** in being a parent, and feeling you're no longer the mother you used to be.
- **Feeling fed up**, as if you can't take any more.

Other common signs women describe:

- You lie awake with lists running through your head.
- Small requests make you disproportionately angry ("Mom, where's my...").
- You feel resentful of your partner's free time, then guilty for feeling that way.
- You can't remember the last thing you did just for fun.
- You get sick more often, or have headaches and tight shoulders most days.
- You fantasize about a hotel room alone. Not a holiday. Just silence.

If several of these feel familiar, you're not weak and you're not failing. Burnout happens when demands stay higher than resources for a long time. It's a signal that something needs to change in the load, not a verdict on you.

## The irritability link

One of the first signs is often a short temper. When your mind is full, your patience is the first thing to go, and the people closest to you get the sharpest edge. If you notice you're yelling more than you want to, that's often the load talking, not your character. (If yelling is a big part of your days, *I Didn't Mean to Yell*, another book in this series, goes deeper into calming down and repairing with your kids.)

## Finishing the stress cycle

In their book *Burnout*, Emily and Amelia Nagoski explain that dealing with the cause of stress and dealing with the stress itself are two different things. You might not be able to change the cause tonight, but you can help your body finish the stress response. Things that help:

- **Moving your body:** a brisk walk, dancing in the kitchen, twenty minutes of anything that gets you a little out of breath.
- **Slow breathing:** in for a count of four, out for a count of six, for a few minutes.
- **A real laugh** with someone, or a long hug (the kind that lasts more than a few seconds).
- **A good cry**, if it comes.
- **Making something:** drawing, baking, gardening, music.

None of these fix the load. They keep you from carrying the stress of it in your body while you work on the load itself.

## When to see a doctor

Please talk to your doctor if:

- you feel low, hopeless or numb most days for two weeks or more,
- you can't sleep, or can't stop sleeping,
- you've lost interest in almost everything,
- you're using alcohol or anything else to get through the evenings,
- you feel you might hurt yourself or that your family would be better off without you.

Some of these can be signs of depression or anxiety, and thyroid problems, low iron and other physical issues can also cause deep tiredness. A check-up is not an overreaction. If you are in crisis, call or text **988** in the US, call Samaritans on **116 123** in the UK and Ireland, or call your local emergency number.

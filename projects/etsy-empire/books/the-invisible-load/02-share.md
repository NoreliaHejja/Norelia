# Chapter 4: The Family Inventory

![](ch2.jpg)

You can't share what nobody can see. Your brain dump from Chapter 1 was the rough draft. Now you'll turn it into a family inventory: a written list of everything it takes to run your home, with a name next to each item.

## Step 1: Sort by area

Take your brain dump and sort the items into areas. Use whatever headings fit your family. Many homes end up with something like this:

- **Food:** meal planning, grocery list, shopping, cooking, lunches, snacks, cleaning up.
- **Laundry and clothes:** washing, folding, putting away, noticing what's outgrown, buying new.
- **House:** tidying, cleaning, repairs, bins and recycling, plants, pets.
- **Kids' school:** forms, emails, homework, supplies, teacher meetings, school events.
- **Kids' health:** doctor and dentist, medicines, vaccinations, sick days.
- **Activities and social life:** clubs, lessons, playdates, birthday parties, gifts.
- **Calendar and travel:** holidays, visits, packing, school breaks, childcare.
- **Money and paperwork:** bills, insurance, subscriptions, taxes, savings.
- **Family and friends:** birthdays, cards, calls to relatives, care for aging parents.
- **Car and transport:** fuel, service, school runs, carpools.

## Step 2: Add the hidden steps

For the five areas that weigh on you most, write the hidden steps next to the visible task, the way you practiced in Chapter 2. "Dentist" becomes: notice it's been six months, find a time, call, put it on the calendar, arrange time off, remind the child, take them, book the next one.

This step matters. Without it, the list looks shorter than the work really is, and the conversation in Chapter 5 turns into "but I do the bins."

## Step 3: Mark who does it now

Next to each item, write who currently carries it. Use three columns if it helps: *who notices*, *who decides*, *who does*. You'll probably see a pattern. Often one person does a lot of the doing, and the other does almost all of the noticing.

## Step 4: Mark what drains you

Put a star next to the items that drain you most. Not the biggest ones, but the ones that make your stomach drop. For one woman it's school emails; for another it's the weekly "what's for dinner." These starred items are your first candidates for handing over, simplifying or dropping.

## What a page might look like

| Task | Who notices | Who decides | Who does | Drains me? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly groceries | Me | Me | Partner | ★ |
| School emails | Me | Me | Me | ★ |
| Bins and recycling | Partner | Partner | Partner | |
| Kids' clothes | Me | Me | Me | |
| Birthday gifts | Me | Me | Me | ★ |

You don't need to make it beautiful. A notebook or a shared document works fine. What matters is that it exists outside your head.

**Practice:** finish the inventory for at least three areas before you have the conversation in Chapter 5. If you share a home with a partner, you can invite them to do their own list first and compare. Many partners are surprised by how long the other list is, and some will find tasks they do that you hadn't counted. That's useful too.

**If it doesn't work:** if the inventory starts to feel like another chore on your list, set a timer for twenty minutes a day for three days. Done is better than complete.

# Chapter 5: The Conversation

This is the chapter many women dread. You've tried talking about it before. It turned into an argument, or a promise that faded in a week, or a hurt silence. So let's plan this one carefully.

## Why these talks go wrong

Most conversations about the load fail for a few predictable reasons:

- **They happen at the worst moment:** at 9 p.m., after a hard day, right after something was forgotten.
- **They start as an accusation:** "You never..." puts anyone on the defensive.
- **They stay vague:** "I need more help" has no clear next step.
- **They end without a decision:** everyone feels better for a day, and nothing changes.

## Choose the moment

Ask for a time, don't ambush. Pick a calm moment when neither of you is hungry or rushing. A weekend morning walk, coffee after the kids are in bed, or a set time on the calendar.

> "I'd like to talk about how we split the running of the house. Nothing's wrong, but I'm feeling stretched and I want us to fix it together. Could we sit down on Sunday morning for half an hour?"

## Open with the problem, not the person

Start with how it feels for you and what you want, not with what they do wrong.

> "I've noticed I'm keeping track of almost everything for the family in my head: appointments, school stuff, food, gifts. Even when you do the tasks, I'm usually the one remembering them and checking they happened. I'm exhausted, and I don't want to feel resentful. I'd like us to share the thinking, not only the doing."

Then show the inventory. Let the list do the talking. Point out the hidden steps behind a couple of starred items.

## Ask, don't prescribe

Give your partner room to respond and choose.

> "Looking at this, which of these would you be willing to take on completely, from noticing to finishing?"

People are more committed to jobs they've chosen. If they pick something you'd rather keep, say so, and keep looking.

## Agree on three handovers

Don't try to rebalance everything at once. Agree on three whole jobs that move from you to your partner, starting this week. Write them down with what "complete" means (Chapter 6 shows how).

## Common replies, and what to say

**"But I do loads around here."**

> "You do, and I see it. This isn't about who works harder. It's about the planning and remembering, which nobody can see, so I don't think either of us has been counting it."

**"Why didn't you just ask me?"**

> "Because asking is part of the work. If I have to notice, decide and ask, I'm still managing it. I want you to own some things so I can forget about them completely."

**"You have higher standards than I do."**

> "Maybe for some things. Let's agree on what 'done' looks like for each job, and then I'll stay out of how you do it."

**"I'm too busy at work."**

> "I get that. Let's pick jobs that fit your week. Some of them, like ordering things online or booking appointments, can be done from your phone in a few minutes."

**Silence, or "Fine, whatever."**

> "I can tell this is hard to hear. I'm not blaming you. I just can't keep going like this. Can we try three changes for one month and then talk again?"

## End with a check-in date

Before you finish, put a short check-in on the calendar for one week later, and another one month later. A quick "how's it going?" keeps the change from fading.

**Practice:** write your opening sentences on a card before the talk. It's much easier to stay calm when you're not looking for words.

**If it doesn't work:** if every attempt ends in a fight, or your partner refuses to discuss it, that's important information. Couples counseling can help you have this conversation with a neutral person in the room. If you feel afraid of your partner's reaction, please reach out to a domestic abuse helpline in your country; you deserve to be safe.

# Chapter 6: Hand Over Whole Jobs

Here's the change that makes the biggest difference: handing over whole jobs, not tasks.

A *task* is "buy the birthday present." A *whole job* is "kids' birthday parties": noticing invitations, replying, putting the party on the calendar, buying and wrapping the present, getting the child there and picking them up. When you hand over only the task, you keep the noticing and the checking, which is the part that wears you out.

Eve Rodsky's book *Fair Play* describes this as owning a job from start to finish, from thinking of it to planning it to doing it. You don't need her full system to use the idea.

## The handover card

For each job you hand over, write a short card together:

- **The job:** e.g., *Weekly groceries.*
- **What it includes:** checking what's running low, keeping the shopping list, ordering or shopping, putting things away.
- **What "done" means:** the fridge has food for the week's meals; we don't run out of milk, bread, coffee or nappies.
- **Timing:** order by Thursday evening, delivery Friday.
- **Where the info lives:** shared shopping list on our phones.

That's it. Five lines, agreed once.

## The two hard rules

**1. Don't remind.** Once a job is handed over, you stop tracking it. If you keep a reminder in your head "just in case," you still own it. If it's missed, the natural result happens (we ate pasta because nobody bought vegetables), and you talk about it at the check-in, not in the moment.

**2. Don't take it back.** If your partner does it differently, or a bit worse at first, resist fixing it. Each time you step in, you teach both of you that the job is still yours. Chapter 10 has more on this.

## Good first jobs to hand over

Start with jobs that are regular, clear and don't need years of history in your head:

- Groceries or online orders
- Bins, recycling and household supplies (soap, batteries, light bulbs)
- One child's after-school activity, completely
- The car: fuel, service, insurance renewal
- Pet care: food, vet, flea treatment
- Weekend breakfast, every weekend
- Packing for trips, or for one person on the trip
- Paying one category of bills

## Jobs that need more care

Some jobs carry a lot of knowledge: kids' medical needs, school relationships, care for an elderly parent. These can be shared too, but plan a proper handover: a written page of key information (doctors, medicines, allergies, contacts), and maybe doing the next appointment together.

## What to say when a handed-over job is missed

Keep it short and about the job, not the person.

> "The school form for the trip didn't go back in time. That's part of the school job now. What do you need so it doesn't slip next time? Would a reminder in your calendar help?"

Then let them solve it. Their system might look nothing like yours. That's fine.

**Practice:** write handover cards for your three agreed jobs this week and stick them somewhere you both see them.

**If it doesn't work:** if a job keeps getting dropped, look at the card together. Is "done" clear? Is the timing realistic? Sometimes the job is too big and needs splitting. Sometimes it needs a different owner.

# Chapter 7: When He Says "Just Tell Me What to Do"

"Just tell me what to do, and I'll do it." Many women hear this, and it's usually meant kindly. It also leaves the hardest part of the work with you.

When you tell someone what to do, you're still the manager. You noticed the need, figured out the options, decided and planned. The other person becomes your assistant, and you still have to check that it happened. That's the whole invisible load, minus the bit that takes ten minutes.

## Why it happens

Often it's not laziness. People who haven't carried the load don't have the habit of noticing. They genuinely don't see the low milk or the expiring passport, because they've never needed to. Noticing is a skill, and like any skill, it grows with practice and with responsibility.

## How to answer it

> "I appreciate that you want to help. The problem is that telling you what to do is the tiring part. I'd love you to take a few areas where you're the one who notices what needs doing, without me telling you. Can we pick them together?"

If they say, "But I don't know what needs doing," you can offer to explain one area in detail, once.

> "Okay. Let me walk you through the kids' clothes for one season: how I check what fits, what we need for school, where I usually buy things. After that, it's yours, and you'll do it your way."

## Build the noticing habit

A few things help a partner learn to see the load:

- **Own an area, not a list.** Owning "the car" builds noticing. Following a list doesn't.
- **A weekly look-ahead together.** Ten minutes on Sunday looking at the week's calendar (Chapter 9). Each of you says what your areas need this week.
- **Questions instead of answers.** When they ask "what should I do about X?", try "what do you think it needs?" before giving your view.
- **Let them build their own system.** Calendar alerts, a whiteboard, an app. Their tool doesn't have to be yours.

## When the pattern doesn't change

If you've had the talk, handed over jobs and still find yourself managing everything months later, it's worth naming it directly:

> "We agreed on these jobs, and I'm still the one remembering them. I can't carry this anymore. What's getting in the way for you?"

Sometimes the answer reveals something real (too many hours at work, not understanding a task). Sometimes it doesn't. Either way, you've made the problem clear, and you can decide your next step, including asking for help from a counselor.

**Practice:** this week, the next time you're asked "what should I do?", answer with a question: "What do you think needs doing?" Then wait.

**If it doesn't work:** if your partner truly can't take on more right now (illness, a crisis at work), make the load lighter in other ways: simplify (Chapter 9), lower the standard (Chapter 10) and bring in other help, paid or unpaid, where you can.

# Chapter 8: Kids Can Carry More Than You Think

![](ch3.jpg)

Many mothers do everything for their children because it's faster. It is faster, for now. But children who help at home learn skills, feel capable and become part of the team. And every small job a child owns is one less thing in your head.

## Why it's hard to start

- It takes longer at first, and you're already short on time.
- The result isn't perfect: towels folded into odd shapes, crumbs left on the table.
- Kids complain, and nagging feels like more work than doing it yourself.

The trick is to treat the first few weeks as training, not as a way to save time. The time comes back later.

## Jobs by age

Every child is different, so use this as a starting point, not a rule. Pick one or two new jobs at a time.

**Ages 2 to 3**
- Put toys in a basket
- Put dirty clothes in the laundry basket
- Help feed a pet (with you)
- Wipe a small spill with a cloth
- Carry their plate to the counter

**Ages 4 to 5**
- Set the table (napkins, cutlery)
- Match socks
- Put away their own clean clothes in a low drawer
- Water plants
- Help unpack groceries
- Get dressed and put shoes by the door

**Ages 6 to 8**
- Make their bed (simply)
- Pack their school bag with a checklist
- Empty the dishwasher cutlery
- Fold towels
- Help make simple snacks or sandwiches
- Feed the pet on their own

**Ages 9 to 12**
- Make their own lunch
- Load and empty the dishwasher
- Run a load of laundry with guidance
- Take out bins and recycling
- Cook a simple meal once a week with help
- Keep track of their own activities on a family calendar

**Teens**
- Do their own laundry
- Cook a family dinner once a week
- Manage their own appointments and school forms (with backup)
- Do one grocery shop or online order
- Look after a younger sibling for short periods

## How to hand a job to a child

1. **Show it once, slowly.** Do it together.
2. **Let them do it while you watch.** Say what's going well.
3. **Let them do it alone.** Check at the end, not during.
4. **Make it part of the routine,** not a favor. "After dinner, you clear the plates" works better than asking each evening.

A picture checklist helps younger children (morning routine, school bag, bedtime) so you're not the one remembering every step.

## What to say

> "You're getting big enough to help run our home. From this week, setting the table is your job. I'll show you tonight, and then it's yours."

When they complain:

> "I know it's not fun. It's still your job. When the table's set, you can go back to playing."

When they do it:

> "Thanks for setting the table. That really helped me."

Keep praise specific and short. You're thanking a team member, not rewarding a trick.

**Practice:** choose one new job for each child this week. Show it once, then let it be theirs.

**If it doesn't work:** if every job becomes a battle, make it smaller and tie it to something that already happens ("plates to the sink before dessert"). For children with ADHD or other needs, short, visual steps and a lot of encouragement make a big difference.

# Chapter 13: Holidays, Birthdays and Big Changes

The invisible load has seasons. For many women the heaviest weeks of the year are not ordinary weeks at all. They're December, the start of the school year, a child's birthday, a house move, a new baby. In these weeks the list doubles, and the old habit of "I'll just do it myself" comes roaring back.

Planning for these peaks ahead of time is one of the kindest things you can do for yourself.

## The holiday load

Think about everything that goes into a family Christmas, Hanukkah, Eid, Thanksgiving or any big celebration: gifts for children, teachers, relatives and friends; cards; food; decorations; travel; school concerts; costumes; hosting; thank-you notes afterwards; and keeping everyone's feelings in mind along the way. Much of it is noticing and remembering, and much of it lands on one person.

**Hold a holiday planning meeting** in early November (or a month before your celebration). Bring a list of everything the season usually involves, with the hidden steps written out. Then go through it together:

- **What can we drop this year?** Fewer gifts, fewer events, a simpler meal, no cards, or digital cards.
- **Who owns what?** Hand over whole holiday jobs: *all gifts for your side of the family*, *the tree and decorations*, *the school's holiday events*, *travel and packing*.
- **What's the budget?** Agree on a number, so nobody spends evenings worrying about money alone.
- **Where's the rest?** Put at least two quiet, plan-free days on the calendar.

**What to say:**

> "Every year I end December completely drained. This year I'd like us to plan it together and split it properly. Could we sit down this weekend and go through the whole list? I'd also like to drop a few things so we actually enjoy it."

## Birthdays

A child's birthday party can hold more hidden steps than any other single event: guest list, invitations, replies, venue, food, cake, party bags, present, thank-yous. Choose one owner for each birthday, or split the party into whole jobs: one person does invitations and guests, the other does food and cake.

And remember: a small party at home, a trip to the park with two friends, or a special family day all count as a good birthday.

## The start of the school year

August and September bring a wave of forms, supplies, uniforms, new schedules, new teachers and new worries. Before term starts:

- Put every key date in the shared calendar the moment you get it.
- Decide who owns school communication for each child (emails, apps, letters).
- Do a uniform and shoe check together, with the child, in one afternoon.
- Set up the command center (Chapter 9) for the new routine.

## Big changes: a new baby, a move, a new job, illness

When life changes, the load grows overnight, and often in ways nobody planned for. A few principles help:

- **Name the season.** "This is a heavy season. For the next three months, we'll do less." Saying it out loud lets everyone lower the bar together.
- **Make a temporary inventory.** List the extra jobs the change brings (baby appointments, moving boxes, new commute) and divide them like any other job.
- **Accept help early.** When people ask "Let me know if I can help," answer with something specific: "Could you bring dinner on Thursday?" "Could you take the big kids to the park on Saturday morning?"
- **Plan the return.** Put a date in the calendar to look again at the split once the change settles. Temporary arrangements have a way of becoming permanent if nobody checks.

After a new baby, especially, the person on leave often ends up holding the whole home, not just the baby. If that's you, Chapter 11 applies twice over. Protected sleep counts as protected time.

**Practice:** look at the calendar for the next three months. Mark the busiest weeks, and decide now what you'll drop or share during them.

**If it doesn't work:** if you always end the holidays exhausted no matter what you plan, try one radically simpler year as an experiment. Ask the children what they really care about. It's often three things, not thirty.

# Chapter 14: Words for Everyone Else

The invisible load doesn't only come from inside your home. Grandparents, schools, other parents and even your own children all send their requests to the person they think is in charge. Usually that's you. Changing who carries the load includes changing who they come to.

These scripts are short on purpose. Use them as they are, or change the words until they sound like you.

## When the school always contacts you

Most schools let you list more than one contact. Ask for both parents (or carers) to receive every message, and agree at home who answers.

> "Could you add my partner to all school emails and the class app, please? He'll be the main contact for trips and forms from now on."

## When relatives always call you to plan

> "Thanks for asking! Tom is organizing our side of the holidays this year, so please check the dates with him."

> "I'd love to see you. Could you message James about the weekend? He has the calendar for Saturdays."

At first, they'll keep coming back to you. Keep redirecting, kindly and every time. It usually takes a few rounds.

## When your children only ask you

Children go to whoever answers. If your partner is in the room, try:

> "Ask Dad. He's in charge of that."

Or, for older kids:

> "Have you checked the calendar? It's all there."

If you're the one they ask about everything, it can help for the other parent to step in visibly for a while: answering questions, signing forms, packing bags. Children learn quickly who to go to.

## When friends or other parents ask you to take on more

The invisible load often grows through small yeses: one more class rep job, one more bake sale, one more carpool. Saying no doesn't need a long reason.

> "Thanks for thinking of me. I can't take that on this term."

> "I can't do the whole thing, but I could bring a cake on the day."

## When someone praises you for "doing it all"

Sometimes the hardest moment is a compliment. "I don't know how you do it all!" can feel like a trap, because the honest answer is: badly, and at a cost. You're allowed to be honest:

> "Honestly? I'm trying to do less of it. We're sharing more at home these days."

Every time you say something like that, you make the invisible load a bit more visible for the women around you too.

## When your partner is praised for "helping"

> "He's great with the kids. And it's our job, so we share it."

You don't need to argue or embarrass anyone. A calm sentence that treats shared work as normal is enough.

**Practice:** this week, redirect one request that would normally come to you. Notice how it feels.

**If it doesn't work:** if people keep ignoring your redirections, make the change official: an email to the school, a short message to the family group ("From now on, Tom is in charge of..."). Written words are harder to forget.
